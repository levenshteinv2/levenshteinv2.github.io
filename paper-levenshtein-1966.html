Levenshtein 1965/1966 Paper: Binary Codes, Edit Distance, and Deletion-Correcting Codes

Levenshtein 1965/1966 Paper: Binary Codes, Edit Distance, and Deletion-Correcting Codes

Vladimir I. Levenshtein's paper Binary Codes Capable of Correcting Deletions, Insertions, and Reversals is the foundational scholarly work behind the sequence metric now known as Levenshtein distance.

It is also one of the most frequently oversimplified papers in the history of string algorithms.

The paper was not written about spelling correction, fuzzy search, natural-language processing, or a dynamic-programming matrix. It was a coding-theory paper about communication channels in which synchronization can be disrupted by deleted or inserted binary symbols.

Its central mathematical ideas include:

  • a deletion-insertion metric on binary words;
  • an equivalence between deletion correction, insertion correction, and mixed insertion/deletion correction for fixed-length codewords;
  • combinatorial estimates for deletion and insertion neighborhoods;
  • asymptotic bounds for the size of deletion/insertion-correcting codes;
  • a use of the Varshamov-Tenengolts congruence construction to correct a single deletion or insertion;
  • the asymptotic result \(L_1(n)\sim 2^n/n\);
  • a second metric incorporating binary-symbol reversals—what modern string terminology would normally call substitutions;
  • bounds for codes simultaneously correcting deletions, insertions, and reversals;
  • and a third section concerned with continuous transmission without external synchronization symbols.

The original Russian article appeared in Doklady Akademii Nauk SSSR, volume 163, issue 4, pages 845–848, in 1965. Math-Net records the submission date as 2 January 1965. The English translation appeared in Soviet Physics–Doklady, volume 10, issue 8, pages 707–710, in February 1966. See the Math-Net bibliographic record.

This explains why the same foundational work is variously cited as:

Levenshtein 1965

or

Levenshtein 1966.

The scientifically precise interpretation is:

1965
=
Russian original

1966
=
English translation.

1. The title of the paper

The English title is:

Binary Codes Capable of Correcting
Deletions, Insertions, and Reversals

2. “Reversal” is historically easy to misread

In modern string-processing language, reversal often suggests an operation such as:

AB
->
BA

or reversing a substring.

3. That is not what Levenshtein means

The paper begins with binary-channel failures:

0 -> 1

1 -> 0.

It calls these operations reversals.

4. In modern string terminology this is a substitution or bit flip

Thus the three operation types relevant to the full metric in the paper correspond to:

deletion

insertion

substitution.

5. Adjacent transposition is absent

The operation:

AB
->
BA

is not one primitive operation in classic Levenshtein distance.

That distinction is developed in Levenshtein vs Damerau-Levenshtein and Hamming Distance.

6. The communication-channel origin

The paper opens from a communication-theory perspective.

A conventional binary channel already allowed:

0 -> 1

1 -> 0.

7. Levenshtein adds synchronization errors

Using the empty word \(\Lambda\), the paper represents deletion as:

0 -> Λ

1 -> Λ

and insertion as:

Λ -> 0

Λ -> 1.

8. This notation exposes the essential distinction

A substitution preserves sequence length.

An insertion or deletion changes it.

9. A substitution preserves later coordinates

ABCDE
ABXDE

Only one position differs.

10. A deletion destroys fixed coordinate synchronization

ABCDE
ABDE.

After C disappears, every later character changes position.

11. Hamming geometry therefore becomes insufficient

Hamming distance assumes an alignment:

position i
against
position i.

12. Levenshtein's channel does not permit that assumption

The receiver may not know where a symbol disappeared or where an extra symbol appeared.

13. The historical problem is synchronization

SUBSTITUTION ERROR
=
wrong symbol

INSERTION / DELETION
=
wrong sequence
+
shifted synchronization.

14. This is why the paper belongs first to coding theory

The famous string metric arises as the geometry appropriate to this channel model.

15. Paper structure

The English translation contains three principal sections:

1.
Codes Capable of Correcting
Deletions and Insertions

2.
Codes that Can Correct
Deletions, Insertions,
and Reversals

3.
Use of Codes for Transmission
Without Synchronizing Symbols
Over Channels that Delete,
Insert, and Reverse.

16. This structure is revealing

Only the first two sections concern block-code distance directly.

The third asks how such codes can be used in a continuous transmitted stream where word boundaries themselves can be corrupted.

17. Binary words

The paper works primarily with words over:

{0,1}.

18. Fixed-length codes

A code is initially defined as an arbitrary set of binary words having one fixed length.

Let:

B_n
=
set of all binary words
of length n.

19. Fixed length is important

Several equivalence arguments in the paper depend on the original codewords having equal length.

20. An overlooked footnote

Levenshtein notes that many of the definitions also make sense for arbitrary alphabets with at least two symbols and even for collections containing words of different lengths.

21. But Lemma 1 does not generally survive arbitrary codeword lengths

This is a subtle restriction that is often lost when the result is summarized informally.

22. Definition of deletion correction

A code K corrects up to s deletions if every possible received binary word can arise from at most one codeword in K after at most s deletions.

23. Unique reconstruction is the criterion

The purpose is not merely to detect corruption.

The receiver must uniquely identify the transmitted codeword.

24. Insertion correction is defined analogously

No received word may be obtainable from two different codewords using at most s insertions.

25. Mixed correction

Levenshtein then considers a received word produced using:

i deletions

and

j insertions

with

i + j <= s.

26. Lemma 1 is one of the paper's most important structural results

For fixed-length codewords:

correct s deletions
        <=>
correct s insertions
        <=>
correct any mixture
of deletions and insertions
whose total number <= s.

27. This equivalence is not obvious

A deletion shortens a word.

An insertion lengthens it.

The corruption processes look asymmetric.

28. The fixed original length connects them

Suppose the same received word z can arise from two codewords x and y, both of length n.

29. Let the first path use

i1 deletions
+
j1 insertions
<= s.

30. Let the second use

i2 deletions
+
j2 insertions
<= s.

31. Equal original lengths constrain the net length changes

j1 - i1
=
j2 - i2.

32. Rearranging

i2 + j1
=
j2 + i1.

33. Their common value is

(i1 + i2 + j1 + j2) / 2.

34. Because each original error budget is at most s

i2 + j1
=
j2 + i1
<= s.

35. The mixed ambiguity therefore implies a pure ambiguity

By deleting inserted symbols or inserting missing symbols appropriately, one obtains a common descendant or common superword of the two codewords within the same error budget.

36. This contradicts pure deletion or pure insertion correction

That proves the equivalence.

37. Why the variable-length warning matters

If x and y start with different lengths, then:

j1 - i1
=
j2 - i2

no longer follows.

38. The proof mechanism breaks

This is why the paper explicitly warns that Lemma 1 is generally false for arbitrary variable-length codebooks.

39. The first metric in the paper is not yet full Levenshtein distance

Levenshtein defines a function:

ρ(x,y)

equal to the smallest number of:

deletions
+
insertions

needed to transform x into y.

40. Substitutions are absent from ρ

Therefore this first metric is what modern terminology would call:

pure insertion-deletion distance

or

insdel distance.

41. Levenshtein states that ρ is a metric

It satisfies the standard metric properties on the relevant word space.

42. Code correction obtains a distance characterization

A fixed-length code corrects up to s insertions/deletions exactly when:

ρ(x,y) > 2s

for every distinct pair:

x,y ∈ K.

43. This is the coding-theoretic minimum-distance principle

Error neighborhoods around different codewords must not overlap.

44. Why the threshold is 2s

If the minimum transformation between two codewords can be split into two paths of at most s operations, a corrupted word can become ambiguous between them.

45. Thus

minimum code distance > 2s

guarantees unique correction within radius s.

46. Modern LCS interpretation

The paper itself does not formulate the result through longest common subsequences, but modern notation gives:

ρ(x,y)
=
|x|
+
|y|
-
2 LCS(x,y)

for unit insertion/deletion cost.

47. For equal length n

ρ(x,y)
=
2(
 n - LCS(x,y)
).

48. This is not classic full Levenshtein distance

A substitution requires:

delete
+
insert
=
2

under ρ.

49. Full modern Levenshtein gives a substitution cost of one

That second metric appears later in the paper.

50. Deletion neighborhoods are nonuniform

Levenshtein next studies how many distinct words can be produced from one binary word after deletions.

51. Define

P_s(x)
=
number of distinct words
obtainable from x
using exactly s deletions.

52. He also defines

Q_s(x)
=
number of distinct words
obtainable from x
using exactly s insertions.

53. Runs become the key statistic

The paper writes:

||x||

for the number of runs in x.

54. A run is a maximal block of identical symbols

Example:

01101

decomposes into:

0 | 11 | 0 | 1

and therefore has:

4 runs.

55. Why runs determine one-deletion descendants

Deleting different copies of the same symbol inside one run produces the same result.

56. Example

0001.

Deleting the first, second, or third zero always produces:

001.

57. Deleting from different runs produces different one-deletion descendants

Therefore:

P_1(x)
=
||x||.

58. This is a fundamental nonuniformity

Two length-n binary words can have radically different deletion-ball sizes.

59. Example

000000

has:

1 run.

60. Alternating word

010101

has:

6 runs.

61. One deletion therefore creates one distinct descendant from the first word but six from the second

Deletion geometry is not regular like ordinary Hamming geometry.

62. Levenshtein derives general deletion bounds

For s deletions:

C(||x|| - s + 1, s)
<=
P_s(x)
<=
C(||x|| + s - 1, s).

63. Interpretation of the upper bound

A deletion result can be described by how many symbols were deleted from each run.

64. Distributing s deletions among the runs gives a stars-and-bars-type count

This yields the upper combinatorial estimate.

65. Interpretation of the lower bound

Delete one symbol from each of s pairwise nonadjacent runs.

66. Those choices produce distinct descendants

Counting such run selections gives the lower bound.

67. The insertion side behaves differently

The paper obtains:

Σ[i=0..s]
C(n,i) 2^(s-i)

<=
Q_s(x)

<=

Σ[i=0..s]
C(n,i) C(s,s-i) 2^(s-i).

68. For s = 1 the bounds coincide

Therefore:

Q_1(x)
=
n + 2.

69. Unlike P1, Q1 is independent of the run structure

This contrast is central:

one-deletion sphere size
depends on x

one-insertion sphere size
depends only on n.

70. Why this matters for coding bounds

Uniform balls support simple sphere-packing arguments.

Nonuniform balls require more careful combinatorial averaging.

71. Levenshtein defines L_s(n)

L_s(n)
=
maximum number of codewords
in a binary length-n code
correcting s deletions/insertions.

72. Lemma 2 gives general asymptotic bounds

For fixed s and:

n -> infinity,

the paper proves, in its asymptotic notation:

2^s (s!)^2 2^n / n^(2s)

≲

L_s(n)

≲

s! 2^n / n^s.

73. The notation ≲ has a specific meaning in the paper

Levenshtein defines:

f(n) ≲ g(n)

to mean:

lim f(n)/g(n) <= 1.

74. And

f(n) ~ g(n)

means:

lim f(n)/g(n) = 1.

75. Lemma 2 is not a sharp general-s result

Its lower and upper powers of n differ:

lower:
n^(-2s)

upper:
n^(-s).

76. But for s = 1 the paper closes the asymptotic gap

This becomes Theorem 1.

77. Proof architecture of Lemma 2

Let K be a maximal deletion/insertion-correcting code.

78. Disjoint deletion descendants give a packing inequality

Σ[x in K] P_s(x)
<=
2^(n-s).

79. Why

Every word produced by s deletions has length:

n-s.

80. Correctability means the descendant sets of different codewords are disjoint

Hence they cannot contain more than all:

2^(n-s)

binary words of that length.

81. Maximality gives a covering-type inequality

If a word in B_n were farther than 2s from every existing codeword, it could be added to the code without violating minimum distance.

82. Therefore radius-2s neighborhoods around a maximal code cover B_n

The paper expresses this using:

R_2s(x)
=
number of words
within distance <= 2s
of x.

83. Hence

Σ[x in K] R_2s(x)
>=
2^n.

84. Runs enter again

The number of binary words of length n with exactly i runs is:

2 C(n-1, i-1).

85. Most long binary words have an intermediate number of runs

Words with extremely few or extremely many runs form a comparatively small tail.

86. Levenshtein uses this concentration to control atypical codewords

The proof chooses a run-count cutoff near:

n/2
-
sqrt(s n ln n).

87. Probability estimates from Feller appear in the proof

This explains why W. Feller's probability text is one of only five references in the paper.

88. Theorem 1 is the main quantitative result of Section 1

L_1(n)
~
2^n / n.

89. This determines the asymptotic size of the best binary single-deletion/insertion-correcting code

Not merely a construction size.

90. The upper asymptotic bound follows from Lemma 2

For s=1:

L_1(n)
≲
2^n/n.

91. What remains is a matching lower construction

Levenshtein proves:

L_1(n)
>=
2^n/(n+1).

92. This is where Varshamov-Tenengolts enters

The paper explicitly says it will use one of the constructions of R. R. Varshamov and G. M. Tenengolts.

The original asymmetric-error paper is available from Math-Net.Ru.

Thus each class corrects:

or

For:

98. The paper contains an explicit decoding argument

This point is important.

The proof is not merely existential.

A code corrects up to s deletions, insertions, and reversals exactly when:

167. Again there is an exponent gap for general s

The short 1965 paper does not solve the full multiple-indel-and-substitution coding problem exactly.

321. Final assessment of the paper

Binary Codes Capable of Correcting Deletions, Insertions, and Reversals is foundational not because it presents the software routine now most often called “the Levenshtein algorithm,” but because it establishes the mathematical geometry that routine later computes.

Levenshtein begins from communication channels where synchronization may be lost. A deleted symbol does not merely create one wrong coordinate; it shifts everything that follows. An inserted symbol does the same in the opposite direction. This destroys the positional model underlying Hamming distance and requires a different notion of sequence separation.

The paper first isolates that problem through the pure deletion-insertion metric:

ρ(x,y)
=
minimum insertions
+
deletions.

It then establishes one of its most elegant structural facts:

for fixed-length codewords,

correcting s deletions,

correcting s insertions,

and correcting any mixture
of at most s insertions/deletions

are equivalent capabilities.

That equivalence makes deletion and insertion coding two views of one sequence geometry.

The next insight is combinatorial. Deletion neighborhoods are not uniform. Their size depends on the number of runs in a binary word:

P_1(x)
=
number of runs in x.

Insertion neighborhoods behave differently:

Q_1(x)
=
n+2.

This nonuniformity forces the proof away from the simplest Hamming-style sphere-packing arguments and toward run-count estimates and asymptotic combinatorics.

From those estimates Levenshtein obtains general bounds for codes correcting a fixed number of synchronization errors. For one deletion/insertion he goes much further.

Using the Varshamov-Tenengolts congruence construction:

Σ[i=1..n] i x_i
≡ a
(mod n+1),

he obtains a family of single-deletion-correcting codes with at least:

2^n/(n+1)

codewords in one syndrome class.

The proof contains a remarkably compact decoder. From the received word's Hamming weight and its weighted-checksum deficiency, the receiver determines whether the missing bit was a zero or one and derives enough run-relative positional information to reconstruct the transmitted codeword.

Combined with the general upper bound, this gives:

L_1(n)
~
2^n/n.

This is a much stronger statement than “VT codes work.” It identifies the first-order asymptotic size of the best possible binary single-deletion/insertion-correcting codes.

The paper then introduces:

r(x,y),

the minimum number of deletions, insertions, and binary reversals required to transform one word into another. Since historical “reversal” here means:

0 <-> 1,

this r is the binary unit-cost insertion-deletion-substitution metric that modern computer science recognizes as classic Levenshtein distance.

The distinction is therefore:

ρ
=
pure insdel distance

r
=
full classic binary
Levenshtein distance.

For the full error model Levenshtein proves one-error code bounds:

2^(n-1)/n
<=
M_1(n)
<=
2^n/(n+1).

Finally, the paper moves beyond isolated block decoding. Its third section asks what happens when codewords are transmitted continuously without external synchronizing symbols. Prefixes and suffixes are introduced so that junction patterns act as separators. Boundary-location errors themselves are treated as effective channel failures, and admissibility is formulated in terms of finite-state decoding.

That final section is particularly revealing because it reconnects the metric to the problem that generated it:

synchronization.

The paper therefore should not be remembered simply as:

the source of
a string-distance formula.

Its actual intellectual structure is:

synchronization channel
        |
        v
deletion/insertion correction
        |
        v
sequence metric
        |
        v
nonuniform edit neighborhoods
        |
        v
asymptotic code bounds
        |
        v

        |
        v
single-deletion decoder
        |
        v
full insertion/deletion/substitution metric
        |
        v
continuous stream synchronization.

Just as important are the things that should not be attributed to it.

The paper does not introduce
adjacent transposition.

It does not give
the canonical Wagner-Fischer
dynamic-programming treatment.

It does not define
a normalized similarity percentage.

It does not model semantics.

It does not prove
exact finite-n VT optimality
for every n.

It does not identify
the minimum edit script
with the actual physical
error history.

Those boundaries make the historical contribution clearer rather than smaller.

Levenshtein's central achievement was to define and exploit a sequence geometry powerful enough to survive its original coding-theory setting. Later researchers could compute it with dynamic programming, accelerate it using threshold algorithms and bit parallelism, compile bounded neighborhoods into automata, index it at scale, and apply the same operation model to text, speech, OCR, biological sequences, databases, and DNA storage.

The metric became universal because the paper's abstraction was narrow and exact:

What transformations are needed
when an ordered sequence
can lose a symbol,
gain a symbol,
or change a symbol?

The answer developed in four pages in 1965 became one of the foundational mathematical objects of modern sequence comparison.

For the scientist and his wider research program, see Vladimir Levenshtein. For the later evolution from coding theory into string algorithms, see History of Levenshtein Distance. The next paper in the computational lineage is Wagner-Fischer 1974: The String-to-String Correction Problem.
