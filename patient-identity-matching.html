Patient Identity Matching

Patient identity matching is the process of determining whether patient records created by different encounters, departments, facilities, health systems, or data sources refer to the same real-world person.

The problem appears superficially similar to ordinary entity resolution and record linkage, but healthcare changes the consequences of error.

A missed match can fragment one patient's clinical history across multiple records.

An incorrect match can associate another person's clinical information with the wrong patient.

The technical objective is therefore not:

find the most similar demographic record

but:

determine whether two observations
represent the same patient

with enough confidence
for the intended healthcare workflow

This distinction is fundamental.

Levenshtein distance can help compare names, addresses, identifiers, and other textual fields, but edit distance is only one source of evidence inside a patient-identity system.

Patient identity is a real-world identity relation. Character similarity is only evidence about one representation of that identity.

What is patient matching?

The Office of the National Coordinator for Health Information Technology defines patient matching in terms of identifying and linking a patient's data within and across health systems so that information belonging to the same patient can be brought together.

Typical demographic matching fields include:

  • given name;
  • family name;
  • middle name or initial;
  • previous names;
  • date of birth;
  • address;
  • telephone number;
  • email address;
  • locally assigned identifiers;
  • other demographic or identity attributes where appropriate.

ONC Patient Identity and Patient Record Matching.

Patient identity matching is not patient similarity

The phrase patient similarity is also used in clinical informatics for questions such as:

Which patients have
similar diagnoses,
phenotypes,
laboratory values,
treatments,
or outcomes?

That is a different problem.

Patient identity matching asks:

Are these records
about the same person?

Clinical patient similarity asks:

How medically similar
are two different patients?

Two patients can have nearly identical disease profiles and still be different people.

Conversely, records for the same patient can contain very different clinical information at different times.

The identity graph

A useful abstraction separates:

REAL PERSON

    │
    ├── Hospital A patient record
    │
    ├── Clinic B patient record
    │
    ├── Laboratory C identifier
    │
    ├── Pharmacy D record
    │
    └── Health information exchange identity

The records are not themselves the patient.

They are information-system representations associated with the patient.

A patient matching system attempts to infer the relationships:

record A ─────┐
record B ─────┼──► same real person
record C ─────┘

Local identifiers are not automatically global identifiers

A medical record number can uniquely identify a record within one assigning organization while having no meaning outside that identifier namespace.

For example:

Hospital A

MRN:
482901


Hospital B

MRN:
482901

does not imply:

same patient

because the same numerical value may have been independently assigned by two institutions.

The identifier must be interpreted together with:

identifier value
+
assigning authority / namespace

FHIR makes the same distinction

In HL7 FHIR, Patient.identifier represents business identifiers associated with the patient.

The identifier of the FHIR resource itself is a different concept from a patient identifier assigned by a hospital or another authority.

HL7 FHIR Patient resource.

Master Patient Index

A Master Patient Index, or MPI, provides identity-management services where multiple patient records or databases must be reconciled.

Conceptually:

LOCAL SYSTEM A
A-10483
       \
        \
LOCAL SYSTEM B
B-77810  ─────► MPI PERSON 391284
        /
       /
LOCAL SYSTEM C
C-20017

The MPI maintains the knowledge that several source identifiers refer to one patient identity.

Enterprise Master Patient Index

The term enterprise master patient index, or EMPI, is commonly used when the identity service spans multiple applications, facilities, hospitals, or organizational units.

Terminology varies between implementations, but the underlying problem remains:

many local identities
        ↓
one enterprise identity graph

An MPI should not erase provenance

A robust architecture distinguishes:

identity resolution

from

source-record provenance

If records:

A-10483
B-77810
C-20017

are determined to refer to the same patient, their source identifiers should normally remain traceable.

The result should conceptually be:

patient identity
     │
     ├── source A identifier
     ├── source B identifier
     └── source C identifier

rather than destructively replacing the source history with one unexplained record.

Three major identity-integrity errors

Healthcare identity management commonly distinguishes several failure patterns.

Duplicate

A duplicate occurs when multiple records are created for the same patient.

REAL PATIENT P

   ├── MRN 1004
   └── MRN 7812

This corresponds closely to an undermerge or false-negative identity-resolution error.

Overlay

An overlay occurs when information belonging to different individuals becomes associated with one patient record or identifier.

PATIENT A ─┐
           ├── MRN 1004
PATIENT B ─┘

This is an especially dangerous form of overmerge because the resulting record can contain commingled clinical information.

Overlap

In enterprise identity management, an overlap can describe the same person carrying different identifiers across separate facilities before those identities are connected at the enterprise level.

The current SAFER Patient Identification guidance explicitly discusses duplicate, overlay, and overlap conditions as identity-integrity errors that organizations should monitor and remediate.

ONC SAFER Guide: Patient Identification.

False positive versus false negative matching

Let:

M =
two records truly belong
to the same patient

and let the matching system decide:

LINK
or
DO NOT LINK
Reality Decision Outcome
Same patient Link True positive
Different patients Do not link True negative
Different patients Link False positive / overmerge risk
Same patient Do not link False negative / duplicate-fragmentation risk

The two errors are not operationally symmetric

In many clinical workflows an erroneous automatic merge can be particularly hazardous because unrelated clinical information can be presented as if it belongs to one patient.

But a missed match also matters because a clinician may not see relevant information that exists in another record.

Therefore there is no universally correct threshold.

The appropriate decision policy depends on:

  • workflow;
  • clinical consequences;
  • availability of human review;
  • ability to reverse a merge;
  • quality of the source data;
  • purpose of the linkage.

A safer decision architecture

Instead of forcing a binary decision for every candidate, use multiple decision regions:

very strong evidence
        ↓
AUTOMATIC MATCH


intermediate evidence
        ↓
POSSIBLE MATCH
MANUAL REVIEW
or
DEFER


strong contradictory evidence
        ↓
NON-MATCH

This resembles the classical three-way decision framework in probabilistic record linkage.

Stage 1: demographic data quality

Matching quality is bounded by the quality of the data presented to the algorithm.

A mathematically sophisticated matcher cannot recover information that was never captured.

Typical problems include:

  • misspelled names;
  • truncated names;
  • names entered into the wrong component field;
  • nicknames instead of another recorded name form;
  • missing middle names;
  • transposed date components;
  • old addresses;
  • shared telephone numbers;
  • typographical errors in email addresses;
  • inconsistent punctuation and whitespace;
  • different scripts or transliterations.

Real duplicate records exhibit substantial demographic disagreement

A multisite study of hundreds of thousands of confirmed duplicate patient-record pairs found substantial discrepancies across key identifying fields, including frequent name errors and mismatches in other demographic data.

The important lesson is not one particular percentage.

It is that:

Records known to belong to the same patient often do not contain identical demographic attributes.

Why Patient Matching Is a Challenge: Research on MPI Data Discrepancies.

Names must support history

A patient may legitimately be represented by:

current family name

previous family name

preferred name

legal name

middle name

initial

transliterated form

A single canonical string:

FIRST + LAST

can discard useful identity history.

See Name, Address and Place Matching.

Do not use one universal name-cleaning rule

Consider:

Mary Ann
Maryann

or:

De La Cruz
Delacruz

or names containing:

hyphens
particles
multiple family names
non-Latin scripts

Removing punctuation, whitespace, or particles can increase recall in some cases while destroying identity evidence in others.

Unicode belongs in the identity pipeline

A patient name can have canonically equivalent Unicode representations.

Therefore:

raw bytes
or
un-normalized code points

should not automatically define the matching sequence.

See Unicode and Grapheme-Safe Levenshtein.

Transliteration should be auxiliary evidence

Cross-script names may require transliteration before character-level comparison.

But transliteration can be:

  • nonunique;
  • language dependent;
  • many-to-one;
  • inconsistent across systems.

A strong architecture keeps:

original script
+
normalized original
+
optional transliteration

rather than replacing the original name permanently.

See Text Normalization and Transliteration.

Date of birth

Date of birth is often highly discriminative, but it should not be treated as infallible.

Possible discrepancies include:

month/day transposition

single-digit typo

default date

estimated date

missing day or month

format conversion error

A comparison model can distinguish:

exact date

same year/month

same year/day

one-component disagreement

complete disagreement

missing

rather than reducing every nonexact comparison to one generic failure.

Addresses are useful and unstable

Addresses are often highly discriminative because they contain several structured components.

But patients move.

Therefore:

different current address
⇏
different patient

and:

same address
⇏
same patient

because family members and other household members can share an address.

Address history is valuable identity evidence

Suppose:

SYSTEM A

Jane Wilson
18 Grove Street


SYSTEM B

Jane Wilson
92 River Road

If historical data show:

Jane Wilson
previously lived at
18 Grove Street

the apparent address disagreement becomes positive longitudinal evidence.

Project US@

Project US@ defines a healthcare-oriented specification for consistent representation of U.S. patient addresses.

Its purpose explicitly includes improving address quality for patient matching, while building on USPS addressing conventions.

Project US@ Technical Specification.

Standardization should precede fuzzy address comparison

Consider:

125 North Jefferson Street Apartment 4

125 N Jefferson St Apt 4

Much of the apparent difference can be removed by structured address standardization.

The matching system should not spend its fuzzy-error budget on differences that are known formatting conventions.

Telephone numbers

Telephone numbers may be strong identifiers when normalized correctly.

But they can also be:

  • reassigned;
  • shared by household members;
  • entered with or without country codes;
  • formatted inconsistently;
  • obsolete.

Useful comparisons may distinguish:

exact normalized number

same national significant number

same suffix

different number

missing

Email addresses

Exact email agreement can be highly discriminative in many populations.

However:

  • accounts can be shared;
  • people can have several addresses;
  • addresses change;
  • typographical errors occur;
  • some systems contain placeholder addresses.

The local part and domain can also be compared separately when appropriate.

Local patient identifiers

An exact identifier match under the correct assigning authority is usually much stronger evidence than approximate demographic similarity.

But identifiers can still be:

  • entered into the wrong namespace;
  • reused incorrectly during migration;
  • missing;
  • mapped incorrectly between systems.

Identity systems should therefore preserve identifier provenance and assignment authority.

Stage 2: candidate generation

Suppose an enterprise MPI contains:

N = 50,000,000
patient identities

A registration event should not normally run every expensive comparator against all 50 million identities.

Instead:

new patient observation
       ↓
candidate generation
       ↓
small plausible identity set
       ↓
detailed comparison

Candidate generation is safety critical

If the correct patient is not placed into the candidate set:

true patient
∉
candidate set

no downstream probabilistic model can recover the match.

Therefore:

end-to-end recall
≤
candidate recall

This is the same structural principle that appears in scalable approximate string retrieval. See Indexing and Filtering for Edit Distance.

Candidate-generation examples

Candidate identities might be retrieved through several independent routes:

exact local identifier

OR

same date of birth
+
surname prefix

OR

same normalized phone

OR

same email

OR

same postcode
+
similar name

OR

historical address
+
date of birth

Use multiple candidate routes

A patient can have an error in any one field.

A union of candidate strategies:

C =
C_identifier
∪
C_name_dob
∪
C_phone
∪
C_address
∪
C_email

reduces dependence on one supposedly stable attribute.

Stage 3: field-level comparators

Once candidates have been retrieved, compare attributes using models appropriate to their semantics.

Field Possible comparator
Patient identifier Exact value + assigning authority
Name Exact, edit distance, Jaro-Winkler, phonetic, alias logic
Date of birth Exact and component-aware disagreement
Address Structured address components + history
Phone Normalized exact / partial comparison
Email Exact or structured textual comparison

Levenshtein distance for patient names

Suppose:

Jonathan Rivera

Jonathon Rivera

The names differ by a small edit.

Levenshtein distance therefore provides useful evidence.

But:

d_L(name₁,name₂) = 1

does not imply:

P(
same patient
|
names
) = high

without considering:

  • name frequency;
  • date of birth;
  • address;
  • telephone;
  • other identifiers;
  • the candidate population.

Short names require special caution

For:

Li
Lu

one edit constitutes a large proportion of the entire string.

For:

Alexanderson
Alexandersson

one edit is much smaller relative to the name length.

Raw edit thresholds should therefore not be applied blindly across every string length.

Transpositions

A data-entry error such as:

Brian
Biran

is naturally interpreted as an adjacent character transposition.

Classic Levenshtein distance does not include transposition as a primitive operation.

See Damerau-Levenshtein Distance.

Weighted edit costs

Some errors occur more frequently than others:

keyboard-neighbor substitution

OCR confusion

missing punctuation

common transliteration difference

A weighted model can encode those differences.

See Weighted Levenshtein Distance.

But arbitrary weights do not automatically produce calibrated patient-match probabilities.

Jaro-Winkler and phonetic methods

Healthcare matching systems frequently use more than one name comparator.

Jaro-Winkler can be useful for short noisy names, while phonetic comparators can capture some pronunciation-related variants.

Their strengths and limitations are developed in Levenshtein vs Jaro-Winkler and Phonetic Matching.

Never let a string comparator decide patient identity by itself

These names can be identical:

Michael Johnson
Michael Johnson

and still describe different patients.

This is the central boundary:

string equality
≠
person identity

See String Similarity vs Semantic Similarity.

Comparison vector

For candidate records:

a
and
b

construct:

γ(a,b)
=
[
 name_similarity,
 dob_comparison,
 address_comparison,
 phone_comparison,
 email_comparison,
 identifier_comparison
]

Example:

name:
very similar

date of birth:
exact

address:
historical agreement

phone:
missing

email:
exact

MRN:

The identity decision should be made from the combined evidence, not from the name score alone.

Deterministic matching

A deterministic system uses explicit rules.

For example:

MATCH if:

exact date of birth

AND

exact normalized phone

AND

(
  exact surname
  OR
  surname edit distance ≤ 1
)

Advantages include:

  • interpretability;
  • predictability;
  • easy auditing;
  • simple operational deployment.

Weaknesses include:

  • threshold cliffs;
  • difficulty modeling missing data;
  • poor adaptation to complex error patterns;
  • large rule sets becoming difficult to maintain.

Exact matching is a special deterministic case

A rule:

first name exact
AND
last name exact
AND
DOB exact

can have excellent precision on some data.

But it will miss patients whose records contain:

  • name changes;
  • spelling errors;
  • abbreviated names;
  • transposed dates;
  • incomplete fields.

Probabilistic patient matching

A probabilistic system treats field agreements and disagreements as evidence about two hypotheses:

M =
same patient

U =
different patients

For comparison pattern:

γ

define:

m(γ)
=
P(γ | M)

u(γ)
=
P(γ | U)

and the likelihood ratio:

Λ(γ)
=
m(γ)
/
u(γ)

or log weight:

w(γ)
=
log(
m(γ)
/
u(γ)
)

Rare agreement can carry more evidence

Exact agreement on:

Smith

is not necessarily as discriminative as exact agreement on a rare surname.

Probabilistic matching can model this distinction.

Missing data need their own state

Suppose:

phone A:
3175550191

phone B:
missing

This is not the same observation as:

phone A:
3175550191

phone B:
2125557700

The first represents missing evidence.

The second represents explicit disagreement.

Conditional dependence remains a limitation

Attributes are not always independent.

For example:

street address
and
postcode

are related.

Multiplying evidence as though every field were conditionally independent can double-count correlated information.

Supervised patient matching

With labeled record pairs, matching becomes a classification problem.

Feature vector:

x(a,b)
=
[
 exact_identifier,
 name_edit_distance,
 jaro_winkler,
 dob_exact,
 address_similarity,
 phone_exact,
 email_exact,
 missingness_flags,
 history_features
]

A model can estimate:

score
=
f(x(a,b))

using methods such as:

  • logistic regression;
  • gradient-boosted trees;
  • random forests;
  • neural architectures;
  • other supervised classifiers.

Training distribution matters

Random nonmatching record pairs are usually very easy negatives.

For example:

different name
different DOB
different city
different phone

But production candidates have usually passed blocking and are deliberately similar.

Training on random negatives can therefore produce an unrealistically easy classification problem.

Training and evaluation should represent:

the actual candidate population

encountered by the MPI.

Referential matching

Referential patient matching supplements source patient records with additional identity information drawn from reference data.

Conceptually:

SOURCE RECORD A
       \
        \
         ► REFERENTIAL IDENTITY
        /
       /
SOURCE RECORD B

Reference information may provide historical:

  • names;
  • addresses;
  • telephone numbers;
  • other identity associations.

Why historical identity information can improve recall

Suppose:

RECORD A

Sarah Adams
14 West Street


RECORD B

Sarah Mitchell
88 North Road

Pairwise comparison appears weak.

Reference history might reveal:

Sarah Mitchell
previous surname:
Adams

previous address:
14 West Street

The identity evidence changes substantially.

Referential matching is not automatically superior

Its performance depends on:

  • reference-data quality;
  • coverage;
  • freshness;
  • population characteristics;
  • privacy and governance;
  • matching logic.

It must be evaluated empirically on the intended population.

Real-world comparison of probabilistic and referential matching

A 2022 JAMIA study evaluated probabilistic and referential approaches against a manually reviewed 30,000-record reference set derived from a health information exchange with tens of millions of registrations.

In that particular study and configuration, referential matching achieved substantially higher sensitivity while retaining very high positive predictive value.

The result should not be generalized into:

referential matching
always beats
probabilistic matching

because algorithm tuning, population, available fields, and reference-data coverage matter.

Grannis et al., 2022.

Digital identity and patient matching are distinct

A modern healthcare identity architecture can also use verified digital identifiers or identity-assurance processes.

This is conceptually different from inferring identity entirely from demographics.

DEMOGRAPHIC MATCHING

name
DOB
address
phone
     ↓
infer identity


VERIFIED DIGITAL IDENTITY

verified identity
+
trusted identifier
     ↓

Higher-assurance identifiers can reduce ambiguity

The current HL7 Interoperable Digital Identity and Patient Matching implementation guide explicitly distinguishes digital identity from patient matching and favors reliable identifiers when those are available, while providing guidance for demographic matching when they are not.

HL7 Interoperable Digital Identity and Patient Matching Implementation Guide.

FHIR Patient $match

FHIR provides a standardized operation for asking an MPI or equivalent identity service to find likely patient matches.

Conceptually:

POST
[base]/Patient/$match

input:
patient demographics

        ↓

MPI algorithm

        ↓

candidate Patient records

The matching algorithm itself is intentionally implementation-specific.

FHIR standardizes the interaction, not one universal patient-matching algorithm.

FHIR Patient $match operation.

FHIR match results can be ranked

The operation can return candidate Patient resources ordered by likely match quality.

FHIR also supports:

search score
+
match-grade

to communicate the MPI's assessment of the result.

A score is not automatically a calibrated probability

A value such as:

0.95

must not automatically be interpreted as:

95% probability
that this is
the same patient

unless the matching implementation explicitly defines and validates that interpretation.

Scores can represent ranking confidence without satisfying probability calibration.

onlyCertainMatches and ambiguity

FHIR's $match operation also recognizes that a request can yield several plausible identities.

This is important because:

multiple plausible candidates

should not automatically become:

choose the top one
and merge

Ambiguity is a legitimate output state

Suppose an MPI finds:

Candidate A:
0.91

Candidate B:
0.90

Candidate C:
0.20

The tiny separation between A and B can be more important than A's absolute score.

A system may need:

candidate margin
+
absolute confidence
+
contradiction checks

before accepting one identity.

One-to-one constraints

Some matching operations have structural constraints.

Suppose records from system A and system B should represent one patient at most once in each system.

Then:

A1 → B1
A2 → B1

may indicate a conflict.

Independent pair scores do not enforce one-to-one identity structure.

Global identity consistency

Consider:

A matches B strongly

B matches C strongly

A contradicts C strongly

Simple transitive closure would merge:

A
B
C

despite the contradiction.

Patient identity systems need safeguards against this type of bridge error.

Do not blindly apply transitivity to noisy pairwise matches

True identity is transitive:

A = B
and
B = C

⇒

A = C

But a classifier's statement:

score(A,B) > threshold

does not have the certainty of mathematical identity.

One false-positive edge can contaminate an entire cluster.

Merge versus link

A patient identity platform should distinguish:

we believe these records
refer to the same patient

from:

we will irreversibly
physically merge the records

A link can often preserve source records and provenance.

A destructive merge may be much harder to reverse.

Use stricter thresholds for irreversible operations

A system can reasonably use:

lower threshold
for candidate retrieval

higher threshold
for probable match

very high threshold
for automatic merge

because the cost of false positives rises as the operation becomes more consequential.

Clinical record content should not casually become the identity key

It can be tempting to use:

  • diagnoses;
  • medications;
  • procedures;
  • laboratory values;

as identity evidence.

This requires extreme caution.

Clinical similarity is not identity, and using sensitive clinical information can introduce circular reasoning, privacy concerns, and bias.

Patient identity should primarily be grounded in appropriate identity and demographic attributes, unless a carefully governed system has a justified reason to do otherwise.

Family members are a difficult case

Family members may share:

  • family name;
  • address;
  • telephone number;
  • insurance information;
  • emergency contacts.

A matcher that gives excessive weight to shared household information can overmerge relatives.

Twins and multiple births

Twins may share:

family name
date of birth
address
telephone
parent information

while differing only in a small number of fields.

This creates exactly the situation where a superficially strong demographic match can be dangerous.

Newborn records

Newborn identity management can be difficult because a permanent name may not yet be available and temporary naming conventions can create very similar records.

The safe lesson is:

When a population naturally contains many near-identical demographic records, matching thresholds and workflow safeguards must reflect that population rather than assuming generic uniqueness.

Name changes

A longitudinal healthcare system may observe:

current surname
≠
historical surname

while all records belong to the same patient.

Retaining previous names can therefore improve matching without forcing overly permissive fuzzy thresholds.

Default and low-information demographics

Some records may contain placeholders or highly nondiscriminative values.

If many records share:

same default date

same placeholder phone

same generic address

those fields can create misleading agreement.

Discriminative power matters

For a field value:

v

)

A rare value is generally more discriminative than a common one.

Data completeness is not enough

A field can be:

99.9% populated

but still have low linkage value if most records contain:

the same few values

Data-linkage fitness depends on:

  • completeness;
  • validity;
  • discriminative power;
  • stability;
  • error patterns.

Historical values can be stronger than increasingly fuzzy matching

Suppose two records differ in both surname and address.

One response is to continually loosen string thresholds.

A better response may be:

retain historical surnames
and
historical addresses

so that legitimate identity transitions can be recognized directly.

Do not assume one attribute is permanently stable

Names can change.

Addresses change.

Phone numbers change.

Email addresses change.

Even dates and government identifiers can be entered incorrectly.

A resilient identity system therefore combines multiple attributes and their history.

  • hiding contradictory attributes;
  • sorting candidates without showing why they matched;

  • allowing merges without independent review.

Organizations may need to determine:

Demographic fields often come from small or predictable domains.

Privacy-preserving linkage requires a threat model, not merely:

Among all possible patient pairs, true matches can be extraordinarily rare.

A classifier that declares:

Precision, recall, candidate recall, and operational error rates are more informative.

If this is:

Gold standards are difficult

Evaluation requires knowing:

which pairs really are
the same patient?

But this ground truth is often created using:

  • manual review;
  • trusted identifiers;
  • previous linkage systems;
  • combined adjudication.

Each method can contain errors.

Manual review must itself be reproducible

A 2022 JAMIA framework emphasized that manually reviewed patient-matching reference sets should document the complete adjudication process, including preprocessing, blocking, reviewers, and evaluation procedures.

Gupta et al., 2022.

Inter-reviewer disagreement matters

If two expert reviewers frequently disagree about whether difficult records represent the same patient, the supposed “ground truth” contains genuine uncertainty.

A strong evaluation therefore records:

  • number of reviewers;
  • review rules;
  • disagreement rate;
  • adjudication process;
  • information exposed to reviewers.

Do not leak the answer into evaluation

Suppose gold-standard labels are created primarily using:

telephone number

and then the evaluated matching model also uses:

telephone number

as its strongest predictor.

Performance can partly reflect the labeling procedure rather than independent evidence of general matching ability.

Stratified evaluation

Aggregate performance can hide vulnerable subpopulations and difficult identity conditions.

Stratify by factors such as:

  • name frequency;
  • record completeness;
  • language and script;
  • source system;
  • age of demographic data;
  • changed surname;
  • changed address;
  • multiple births;
  • duplicate-record history;
  • amount of corruption.

Source-specific evaluation

A matcher can perform well overall while failing badly for one hospital whose registration system truncates names or formats addresses differently.

Therefore evaluate:

Summary

Patient identity matching links healthcare records that belong to the same real-world person across encounters, databases, facilities, and organizations. It is a specialized form of entity resolution in which identity errors can directly affect clinical information integrity, interoperability, privacy, operations, and patient safety.

An MPI or EMPI provides the identity layer that maps source-system patient identifiers to resolved patient identities while preserving the provenance of those source records. The objective is not merely to remove duplicate rows; it is to maintain a coherent identity graph across systems.

Identity-integrity errors include duplicates, overlays, and enterprise overlaps. A duplicate fragments one patient's information into multiple records. An overlay can commingle records belonging to different people. These errors represent different sides of the false-negative and false-positive matching problem and require different remediation strategies.

Demographic data quality is a primary determinant of match quality. Names are misspelled and change over time. Addresses change. Phones and emails become obsolete or can be shared. Dates of birth can contain entry errors. Local identifiers are meaningful only within the correct assigning namespace. A patient identity model therefore needs multiple attributes and, when available, longitudinal attribute history.

Standardization should precede fuzzy comparison. Unicode normalization, field-specific name handling, structured address representation, telephone normalization, and identifier namespace validation remove avoidable representational differences before approximate matching is attempted. Project US@ illustrates how address standardization can be designed specifically to support patient matching.

Candidate generation is another critical stage. At enterprise scale, the matcher should not exhaustively compare every new observation with every identity. High-recall candidate retrieval narrows the population using several independent routes. Because a true patient omitted from the candidate set cannot be recovered later, candidate recall places an upper bound on total system recall.

Field-level comparison can then use exact matching, Levenshtein distance, transposition-aware distance, Jaro-Winkler, phonetic methods, structured date and address comparison, historical attributes, and trusted identifiers. Each comparator should model a specific type of discrepancy rather than being treated as a universal identity function.

Probabilistic patient matching combines comparison evidence according to how frequently each pattern occurs among true matches and true non-matches. Supervised machine-learning systems can learn more complex relationships, while referential systems can incorporate historical identity evidence from reference data. No approach should be assumed superior without validation on the intended population.

Modern healthcare identity architecture also distinguishes demographic matching from verified digital identity. When trustworthy digital identifiers are available, they can provide stronger and less ambiguous evidence than demographic inference alone. HL7's current identity-matching work reflects this distinction while continuing to standardize demographic patient-matching workflows.

FHIR's Patient/$match operation standardizes how an application can request candidate matches from an MPI. It does not prescribe one matching algorithm. The MPI can return ranked candidate records and match-quality information, preserving ambiguity rather than requiring every request to produce one supposedly certain patient.

Threshold selection is a safety and operational decision as well as a statistical one. Candidate retrieval can use permissive rules to preserve recall. Automatic links require stronger evidence. Irreversible merges may need an even higher threshold and explicit contradiction checks. Ambiguous cases should remain ambiguous until additional evidence or manual review resolves them.

Evaluation should measure precision, recall, candidate recall, and operational identity errors rather than overall classification accuracy alone. Gold-standard record pairs must themselves be created carefully, and manual adjudication procedures should be reproducible. Performance should also be stratified across data sources, languages, scripts, naming patterns, demographic completeness, and other conditions relevant to the population.

Finally, patient identity matching is not only an algorithmic problem. Registration workflow, user-interface design, demographic capture, system migrations, review queues, model versioning, audit history, reversible remediation, and ongoing monitoring all influence identity integrity.

The correct conceptual architecture is:

accurate data capture

→ consistent representation

→ high-recall candidate generation

→ field-specific comparison

→ evidence combination

→ risk-aware decision

→ provenance-preserving identity graph

→ continuous monitoring
and remediation

Within that architecture, Levenshtein distance has a precise and valuable role: it measures character-level discrepancy in selected textual fields. Patient identity emerges only when that evidence is interpreted together with the rest of the identity record.
