Entity Resolution and Record Linkage

Entity resolution and record linkage ask a deceptively difficult question:

Do two records describe
the same real-world entity?

The records may contain names, addresses, dates, identifiers, telephone numbers, organizations, product attributes, or other fields. Values can be incomplete, misspelled, reformatted, transliterated, outdated, duplicated, or simply wrong.

A typical pair might look like:

RECORD A

name:
Jonathan A. Smith

date_of_birth:
1984-07-16

address:
42 West Harrison Street

postcode:
60605


RECORD B

name:
Jonathon Smith

date_of_birth:
1984-07-16

address:
42 W Harrison St

postcode:
60605

The names are not identical. The addresses are not identical. Yet the combined evidence may strongly suggest that the records refer to the same person.

This is where string similarity, including Levenshtein distance, becomes useful—but only as one component of a larger inference problem.

The central principle of this article is:

String similarity is evidence about field values. It is not proof of entity identity.

A reliable entity-resolution system therefore separates several problems:

raw records
    ↓
standardization
    ↓
candidate generation
    ↓
field comparisons
    ↓
evidence combination
    ↓
pairwise decision
    ↓
optional global consistency
    ↓
entities / linked records

Treating Levenshtein distance as the entire matching algorithm collapses these distinct stages into one number and usually produces poor decisions.

Terminology: record linkage, deduplication, and entity resolution

These terms overlap but are not always identical.

Record linkage

Record linkage traditionally refers to identifying records in different files that correspond to the same underlying entity.

FILE A            FILE B

a₁                b₁
a₂       ↔        b₂
a₃                b₃
...               ...

For example:

hospital registry
        ↔
insurance database

or:

voter file
        ↔
survey respondents

Deduplication

Deduplication applies essentially the same reasoning within one collection:

record 172
and
record 9831

may describe
the same entity

Entity resolution

Entity resolution is often used more broadly for the complete process of determining which observed records belong to which latent real-world entities.

It may involve:

  • cross-file linkage;
  • within-file deduplication;
  • multi-source integration;
  • cluster formation;
  • canonical entity construction.

The terminology varies across statistics, databases, information retrieval, public health, knowledge graphs, and machine learning. What matters computationally is the structure of the matching problem.

The latent-entity view

Suppose observed records are:

r₁, r₂, ..., rₙ

but the real world contains latent entities:

e₁, e₂, ..., eₖ

with:

k ≤ n

Entity resolution seeks an assignment:

zᵢ ∈ {1,...,k}

where:

zᵢ = j

means that record rᵢ refers to entity eⱼ.

Pairwise record linkage instead often focuses first on:

Mᵢⱼ =
1 if records i and j match
0 otherwise

The two formulations are related but not identical. Pairwise predictions can violate the global structure required by an entity partition.

The fundamental distinction: similarity is not identity

Consider:

"John Smith"
"Jon Smith"

Their character strings are close.

But millions of people can share those names.

Now consider:

"Aleksandr Sergeyevich Pushkin"
"Alexander Pushkin"

The strings may differ substantially, yet domain knowledge, transliteration, dates, locations, and other attributes could establish that both records describe the same person.

Thus:

small edit distance
        ⇏
same entity


large edit distance
        ⇏
different entity

This is a special case of the wider distinction developed in String Similarity vs Semantic Similarity.

Entity resolution is an evidence-combination problem

Suppose two person records provide:

name
date of birth
street
postcode
email
telephone

Each field contributes a different kind of evidence.

Observation Possible interpretation
Same exact email Potentially very strong positive evidence
Same surname Strength depends heavily on surname frequency
Name edit distance = 1 Evidence of lexical similarity, not identity
Different birth dates Could be strong negative evidence or one data-entry error
Same postcode Weak or moderate evidence depending on population density
Missing telephone Absence of evidence, not necessarily evidence of non-match

The job of the linkage model is to combine these observations coherently.

Stage 1: standardization

Raw field values often differ for reasons unrelated to identity.

For example:

"42 West Harrison Street"
"42 W. Harrison St."
"42 W HARRISON ST"

A normalization pipeline might standardize:

  • case;
  • punctuation;
  • whitespace;
  • abbreviations;
  • Unicode representation;
  • date formats;
  • telephone formats;
  • known address conventions.

But normalization changes the strings on which similarity is calculated.

Therefore:

normalize
then compare

is mathematically a different operation from:

compare raw values

The transformation policy must be explicit and reproducible.

Multilingual names and transliteration require additional caution; these issues are treated more generally in Text Normalization and Transliteration.

Over-normalization can destroy evidence

Consider identifiers:

AB-120
AB120

Removing punctuation may be harmless.

But for another field:

Unit 12
12

aggressive token deletion may remove meaningful structure.

Normalization should therefore be field-specific rather than one universal text-cleaning function.

Stage 2: candidate generation

For two files containing:

n
and
m

records, exhaustive cross-file comparison requires:

n × m

candidate pairs.

If:

n = 10,000,000
m = 10,000,000

then:

10^14

possible pairs exist.

Pairwise comparison alone is therefore not a scalable architecture.

Blocking

Blocking creates candidate subsets using inexpensive conditions.

For example:

compare records only if

same birth year

OR

same postcode prefix

OR

same surname initial

The objective is:

retain nearly all true matches

while eliminating
the overwhelming majority
of impossible pairs

Blocking is a recall-sensitive filter

Suppose two true records contain:

postcode A:
60605

postcode B:
60606

because one postcode was entered incorrectly.

If the only blocking rule is:

postcode must match exactly

the pair is never evaluated by the sophisticated linkage model.

Its probability of matching downstream becomes irrelevant because:

candidate recall = 0
for that pair

This is analogous to exact candidate-generation requirements in large-scale edit-distance search. See Indexing and Filtering for Edit Distance.

Multiple blocking rules

A safer strategy frequently uses a union:

C =
C₁
∪
C₂
∪
C₃
...

where each Cᵢ comes from a different blocking condition.

For example:

RULE 1
same date of birth

RULE 2
same postcode
and similar surname

RULE 3
same telephone suffix

RULE 4
same rare surname
and same city

A true pair lost by one rule may survive another.

Candidate generation and matching must be evaluated separately

Suppose:

true matches = 10,000

but blocking retains only:

9,500

true matches.

Even a perfect classifier can recover at most:

95%

of all true matches.

Therefore:

end-to-end recall
≤
blocking recall

Stage 3: field comparison

For each candidate pair:

(a,b)

the system constructs comparison features.

Example:

Field Record A Record B Comparison
Name Jonathon Smith Jonathan Smith Edit distance 1
Date of birth 1984-07-16 1984-07-16 Exact agreement
Street 42 W Harrison St 42 West Harrison Street Normalized similarity
Postcode 60605 60605 Exact agreement

The comparison vector might be represented as:

γ(a,b)
=
(
    name_similarity,
    dob_agreement,
    address_similarity,
    postcode_agreement
)

Levenshtein distance as a field comparator

For name fields:

x =
"Jonathon Smith"

y =
"Jonathan Smith"

we may compute:

d_L(x,y) = 1

This is useful information.

But the number:

1

does not itself tell us:

P(
same person
|
d_L = 1
)

because that probability depends on:

  • name frequency;
  • other fields;
  • the data-generating process;
  • the candidate-generation mechanism;
  • the population being linked.

Distance can be discretized into comparison levels

Instead of feeding raw distance directly into a probabilistic model, a system may use levels such as:

level 0:
exact agreement

level 1:
distance = 1

level 2:
distance = 2

level 3:
normalized similarity ≥ 0.8

level 4:
otherwise disagree

Each level can then receive its own statistical evidence weight.

Length matters

An edit distance of 1 has very different meaning for:

"Li"
→
"Lu"

than:

"Alexandrovich"
→
"Alexandrovit"

This is one reason normalized or length-stratified comparison levels may be useful.

However, normalized edit similarity is an application-defined transformation, not the original Levenshtein metric.

Weighted edit distance

Some errors are more plausible than others.

For example:

0 ↔ O

1 ↔ I

rn ↔ m

or neighboring keyboard characters may occur more frequently than arbitrary substitutions.

A domain-specific comparator can use Weighted Levenshtein Distance.

But learned or manually selected costs should not be interpreted directly as match probabilities unless a probabilistic model explicitly gives them that meaning.

Different fields require different comparators

A generic edit-distance comparator is inappropriate for every attribute.

Field Possible comparator
Name Edit distance, Jaro–Winkler, phonetic models
Date of birth Exact match, component comparison, date difference
Latitude/longitude Geographic distance
Telephone Exact normalized number, suffix agreement
Email Exact match, local/domain decomposition
Address Structured address comparison
Numeric amount Absolute or relative numeric difference

The comparative behavior of Levenshtein, Jaro–Winkler, and phonetic methods is treated separately in Levenshtein vs Jaro–Winkler and Phonetic Matching.

The Fellegi–Sunter framework

The classical probabilistic foundation of record linkage was formalized by Ivan Fellegi and Alan Sunter in 1969.

For a candidate pair, let:

M =
pair is a true match

U =
pair is a true non-match

and let:

γ

be the observed comparison pattern.

Define:

m(γ)
=
P(γ | M)

and:

u(γ)
=
P(γ | U)

The evidence supplied by comparison pattern γ can be expressed through the likelihood ratio:

Λ(γ)
=
m(γ)
/
u(γ)

or log weight:

w(γ)
=
log(
    m(γ)
    /
    u(γ)
)

A comparison pattern is strong positive evidence when it is:

common among true matches

but

rare among non-matches

Why rarity matters

Suppose two records agree on surname:

"Smith"

and another pair agrees on:

"Quenel"

Exact agreement occurs in both cases.

But if the first surname is extremely common and the second very rare, the evidential strength is not equal.

Conceptually:

rare agreement
→
large m/u ratio

common agreement
→
smaller m/u ratio

This is one of the central advantages of probabilistic linkage over a rule such as:

+1 point
for every agreeing field

Field-level Fellegi–Sunter weights

Suppose comparisons are divided into fields:

γ =
(
γ_name,
γ_dob,
γ_address,
γ_postcode
)

Under a conditional-independence approximation:

P(γ | M)
≈
∏ᵢ P(γᵢ | M)

and:

P(γ | U)
≈
∏ᵢ P(γᵢ | U)

Therefore:

log
P(γ | M)
─────────
P(γ | U)

≈

Σᵢ
log
P(γᵢ | M)
──────────
P(γᵢ | U)

This gives additive comparison weights.

Worked probabilistic example

Suppose the estimated comparison probabilities are:

Observation m u m/u
Very similar name 0.80 0.02 40
Exact date of birth 0.95 0.003 316.7
Same postcode 0.70 0.04 17.5

The approximate combined Bayes factor under conditional independence is:

40
×
316.7
×
17.5

≈
221,690

That is far stronger evidence than simply saying:

three fields look similar

Prior probability matters

Evidence must be interpreted relative to how likely two candidate records were to match before examining their comparison features.

Let:

π =
P(M)

Then prior odds are:

π
─────
1 - π

Posterior odds can be written conceptually as:

posterior odds
=
prior odds
×
Bayes factor

If two enormous files are cross-joined randomly, the prior probability that an arbitrary pair describes the same person may be extraordinarily small.

Blocking changes the candidate population and therefore changes the effective prior match prevalence among evaluated pairs.

Blocking and probability cannot be conceptually separated

Suppose candidate generation only allows records sharing:

birth year

Then the evaluated pair population is no longer a random sample of all possible record pairs.

The distribution:

P(γ | U)

among blocked non-matches can be very different from its distribution across arbitrary pairs.

Model estimation and evaluation should reflect the actual candidate-generation process.

The original three-way decision rule

Fellegi and Sunter formulated decisions as:

LINK

POSSIBLE LINK

NON-LINK

rather than forcing every pair into a binary classification.

Conceptually:

high score
   ↓
MATCH


intermediate score
   ↓
REVIEW / UNRESOLVED


low score
   ↓
NON-MATCH

This remains extremely useful in high-stakes applications.

Clerical review is a decision region

Human review should not be treated merely as:

send random difficult cases
to people

Instead it can be modeled as a deliberate uncertainty region:

score ≥ T_match
→ automatic match

score ≤ T_nonmatch
→ automatic non-match

otherwise
→ review

Thresholds depend on the cost of:

  • false links;
  • missed links;
  • human review.

False matches and missed matches can have asymmetric costs

For some tasks:

false link
≫
missed link

because incorrectly merging two people contaminates downstream records.

In other tasks:

missed link
≫
false link

because failure to connect relevant observations has a higher operational cost.

There is therefore no universal score threshold.

Estimating m and u probabilities

The probabilities:

m =
P(comparison | match)

u =
P(comparison | non-match)

may be estimated using:

  • labeled match/non-match pairs;
  • high-confidence rules;
  • mixture models;
  • Expectation-Maximization;
  • semi-supervised procedures;
  • domain-specific estimation.

Mixture-model view

Candidate comparison vectors can be viewed as arising from a mixture:

P(γ)
=
π P(γ | M)
+
(1-π) P(γ | U)

where the true class:

M or U

is latent.

Expectation-Maximization can estimate parameters in suitable probabilistic linkage models without complete labeled data.

Conditional independence is convenient but often false

Consider:

city
and
postcode

or:

first name
and
sex

These fields may be strongly dependent.

If a model simply multiplies field likelihood ratios as though every comparison were conditionally independent, evidence can be double-counted.

For example:

same postcode
+
same city

does not necessarily provide the same amount of independent evidence as:

same postcode
+
same date of birth

Dependence is one limitation of simple Fellegi–Sunter implementations

Possible remedies include:

  • joint comparison levels;
  • interaction terms;
  • logistic models;
  • tree-based classifiers;
  • Bayesian hierarchical models;
  • neural comparison architectures.

But greater model flexibility requires more data and creates additional calibration and interpretability problems.

Missing values are not ordinary disagreement

Suppose:

record A telephone:
+1 312 555 0184

record B telephone:
missing

Treating this as:

telephone disagrees

can generate unjustified negative evidence.

A missing field may require a separate comparison level:

missing / unavailable

rather than agreement or disagreement.

Missingness itself can be informative

The situation becomes more complicated when:

P(missing | M)
≠
P(missing | U)

or when missingness depends on source, population, or entity characteristics.

Thus:

"ignore missing fields"

is not universally statistically correct either.

Frequency-sensitive comparisons

A powerful refinement uses the frequency of the actual agreed value.

Compare:

surname = Smith

with:

surname = Zabriskie

Even when both are exact agreements, the rare value can carry more discriminative evidence.

This general principle extends to:

  • names;
  • addresses;
  • postcodes;
  • occupations;
  • organization names.

Value frequency and edit distance interact

Suppose:

"Smith"
"Smth"

and:

"Quenel"
"Quenell"

both have small edit distance.

Their match evidence can still differ substantially because:

string similarity
+
population frequency
+
other attributes

must be interpreted jointly.

Deterministic linkage

Not every linkage system is probabilistic.

A deterministic rule might say:

MATCH if:

exact date of birth

AND

(
    exact normalized surname
    OR
    name edit distance ≤ 1
)

AND

same postcode

Such systems can be:

  • transparent;
  • easy to deploy;
  • easy to audit;
  • effective in clean domains.

But hard thresholds can behave discontinuously.

Threshold cliff

Suppose:

accept name distance ≤ 2

Then:

distance = 2
→ acceptable

distance = 3
→ unacceptable

even if every other field strongly agrees.

A probabilistic model can instead let the name comparison contribute graded evidence.

Supervised classification

With labeled training pairs:

(comparison_vector,
 match_label)

entity resolution can be formulated as classification.

Possible models include:

  • logistic regression;
  • random forests;
  • gradient-boosted trees;
  • support-vector machines;
  • neural networks.

The input can still contain classical field comparators such as Levenshtein distance.

Feature example

x(a,b) =
[
    name_edit_distance,
    normalized_name_similarity,
    dob_exact,
    postcode_exact,
    address_token_similarity,
    email_exact,
    telephone_suffix_match
]

A classifier estimates:

P(
    M = 1
    |
    x(a,b)
)

or another decision score.

Labeled pair sampling is dangerous

Suppose training non-matches are generated by selecting random record pairs.

Most will be trivially different:

different name
different city
different date of birth
different address

A model trained on these easy negatives may perform poorly on production candidates, because blocking deliberately selects difficult pairs that already share some attributes.

Training and evaluation data should resemble the candidate distribution encountered at inference time.

Pairwise probability is not the whole entity-resolution problem

Suppose:

P(A=B) = 0.95

P(B=C) = 0.95

P(A=C) = 0.05

Independent pair decisions can produce:

A = B

B = C

A ≠ C

which is incompatible with ordinary identity because equality is transitive.

Identity is an equivalence relation

For ordinary deduplication:

A = A

A = B ⇒ B = A

A = B and B = C
⇒
A = C

Pairwise classifiers do not automatically enforce this structure.

Clustering

One common approach constructs a graph:

records = vertices

likely matches = edges

and derives entity clusters from the graph.

But simple connected components can overmerge.

Bridge-error problem

Suppose:

A ── B ── C ── D

where only one weak incorrect edge:

B ── C

connects two genuinely different groups.

Connected-components clustering merges:

A
B
C
D

into one entity.

Thus a single false-positive edge can create a much larger entity-level error.

Bipartite linkage has structural constraints

Suppose two files are known to contain at most one record per person.

Then record:

aᵢ

should match at most one:

bⱼ

and vice versa.

Independent pairwise probabilities can violate this one-to-one constraint.

Global bipartite matching

The pair scores can instead be considered jointly.

Suppose:

           B1    B2

A1        0.91  0.89

A2        0.90  0.20

Greedily choosing the largest score first gives:

A1 ↔ B1

and leaves:

A2 ↔ B2

with a poor score.

A global assignment may instead prefer:

A1 ↔ B2

A2 ↔ B1

depending on the model and objective.

Bayesian bipartite linkage

Modern Bayesian formulations can treat the matching structure itself as the parameter of interest rather than assuming independent pair statuses.

This makes it possible to represent:

  • one-to-one matching constraints;
  • posterior uncertainty;
  • unresolved matches;
  • different loss functions for final decisions.

Mauricio Sadinle's Bayesian bipartite framework is an important example of this approach.

Uncertainty should survive the linkage pipeline

A common anti-pattern is:

probabilistic linkage
        ↓
hard matched table
        ↓
pretend linkage was perfect
        ↓
downstream statistical analysis

If linkage errors are non-negligible, downstream estimates can be biased.

In statistical applications it can be important to propagate:

linkage uncertainty

into:

subsequent inference

Entity resolution can be iterative

Initial high-confidence links can improve later decisions.

For example:

high-confidence matches
        ↓
learn source-specific errors
        ↓
improve comparator parameters
        ↓
resolve harder pairs

But self-training can also amplify mistakes.

Error propagation

Suppose an incorrect high-confidence match is accepted early.

If its values are then used to:

  • train error models;
  • construct canonical entities;
  • generate new blocking keys;
  • infer household relationships;

one pairwise mistake can affect many later decisions.

Canonical records are a separate problem

After several records have been assigned to one entity:

{
  "Jon Smith",
  "Jonathan Smith",
  "Jonathan A Smith"
}

the system may need to create a canonical representation.

Possible policies include:

  • most recent value;
  • most frequent value;
  • trusted-source priority;
  • probabilistic latent value;
  • retain all observed variants.

Entity resolution and canonicalization should not be conflated.

Evaluation requires ground truth

When true match labels are available, pairwise evaluation can use:

TP = true matched pairs predicted match

FP = non-matched pairs predicted match

FN = true matched pairs missed

TN = non-matched pairs rejected

Precision

precision
=
TP
───────
TP + FP

answers:

Of predicted matches,
how many were correct?

Recall

recall
=
TP
───────
TP + FN

answers:

Of true matches,
how many were recovered?

F1 score

F1
=
2 × precision × recall
──────────────────────
precision + recall

provides one balance between precision and recall.

But F1 implicitly assigns a particular relative importance to the two error types and may not reflect operational cost.

Evaluate blocking separately

Let:

T =
all true matching pairs

and:

C =
candidate pairs produced by blocking

Then blocking recall is:

|T ∩ C|
───────
|T|

The reduction ratio can be measured against the full pair space.

A useful blocking method seeks:

very high recall

and

very large pair reduction

Pairwise metrics are not sufficient for clustering

A clustering system can produce excellent pairwise scores while still creating poor entity groups.

Cluster-level diagnostics can include:

  • entity-count error;
  • cluster precision and recall;
  • overmerge rate;
  • undermerge rate;
  • cluster-size distortion.

Overmerging

Overmerging occurs when records from different real entities are assigned to one entity.

true:

{A,B}
{C,D}

predicted:

{A,B,C,D}

Undermerging

Undermerging occurs when records from one real entity remain split:

true:

{A,B,C,D}

predicted:

{A,B}
{C,D}

These errors have different consequences and should be reported separately.

Ground truth is often imperfect

Entity-resolution benchmarks frequently rely on labels produced by:

  • manual review;
  • existing identifiers;
  • historical linkage systems;
  • semi-automated labeling.

Those labels can themselves contain errors.

A reported:

precision = 99.8%

should therefore be interpreted relative to the quality and representativeness of the reference labels.

Evaluation must be stratified

Aggregate metrics can hide severe weaknesses.

Useful strata include:

  • common versus rare names;
  • complete versus missing records;
  • short versus long names;
  • language or script;
  • source system;
  • geographic region;
  • record age;
  • amount of corruption.

Why demographic and linguistic variation matters

Different populations can have different:

  • name-length distributions;
  • surname frequencies;
  • transliteration patterns;
  • address structures;
  • missing-data patterns;
  • household naming conventions.

A comparator or threshold calibrated on one population can perform differently on another.

Identity signals are domain-specific

For people:

name
birth date
address
telephone

may be useful.

For organizations:

company name
registration number
address
website
industry

may be relevant.

For products:

SKU
manufacturer
model number
title
dimensions

may matter.

The statistical structure of linkage changes with the entity type.

Names and addresses deserve their own treatment

Names, addresses, and places have complex error modes involving:

  • abbreviations;
  • token reordering;
  • nicknames;
  • transliteration;
  • street conventions;
  • geographic hierarchy.

These issues are developed in Name, Address and Place Matching.

Patient identity has stronger safety constraints

In healthcare, a false merge can incorrectly combine medical histories, while a missed match can fragment one patient's record.

The relative costs are therefore very different from ordinary marketing deduplication.

See Patient Identity Matching.

Privacy changes the matching architecture

Sometimes two organizations need to identify common entities without revealing raw identifiers to each other.

This creates the privacy-preserving record-linkage problem:

Organization A
has identifiers

Organization B
has identifiers

but

raw identifiers
must not be exchanged

Cryptographic, encoded, or privacy-enhancing comparison mechanisms create an additional trade-off between:

  • linkage accuracy;
  • information leakage;
  • computational cost;
  • attack resistance.

See Privacy-Preserving Record Linkage.

Common failure mode: one global Levenshtein threshold

A naive system might define:

same entity
if
Levenshtein(nameA,nameB) ≤ 2

This fails because:

  • names are not unique identifiers;
  • short and long names behave differently;
  • different scripts and transliterations behave differently;
  • other attributes are ignored;
  • common names create large collision populations.

Common failure mode: averaging unrelated similarities

Suppose:

name similarity      = 0.9
address similarity   = 0.8
birth-date similarity = 0
email similarity     = 1.0

Computing:

(0.9 + 0.8 + 0 + 1.0) / 4
=
0.675

has no probabilistic interpretation unless the aggregation rule has been justified.

Different fields have different reliability and discriminative power.

Common failure mode: exact equality is not always stronger than fuzzy agreement

Exact agreement on:

surname = Smith

may be weaker evidence than near agreement on a rare full name combined with exact date of birth.

Evidence strength depends on probability, not merely comparator category.

Common failure mode: treating all disagreements equally

These discrepancies are not equivalent:

birth date differs by one transposed digit

versus

birth dates differ by twenty years

Nor are:

"Jonathan"
"Jonathon"

and:

"Jonathan"
"Mohammed"

A useful comparison system preserves severity information.

Common failure mode: ignoring field quality

Suppose one source's postcode field is known to be:

99.9% accurate

while another source's free-text address is:

frequently stale

Their disagreements should not automatically receive equal negative weight.

Common failure mode: blocking on corrupted fields

A sophisticated model cannot recover candidate pairs that blocking permanently removed.

Blocking should therefore use:

  • multiple keys;
  • robust representations;
  • high-recall rules;
  • measured candidate recall.

Common failure mode: treating pair predictions as independent entities

Identity constraints can be violated if pair decisions are made independently.

This is especially dangerous in deduplication and multi-source clustering.

Common failure mode: using transitive closure blindly

If:

A ~ B
B ~ C

simple connected components imply:

A ~ C

even if:

A and C
have strong contradictory evidence

Global entity construction needs stronger reasoning than graph connectivity alone.

Common failure mode: assuming a high match score is calibrated probability

A model score:

0.97

is not necessarily:

97% probability
of a true match

unless calibration has been validated.

Common failure mode: evaluating only easy pairs

If test non-matches are random cross-file pairs, the task can appear nearly perfect because most pairs are obviously unrelated.

Evaluation should include the difficult candidate population produced by the actual blocking process.

Common failure mode: leakage from identifiers

Suppose ground truth was constructed using:

email address

and the model then uses that same email field as a feature.

Measured performance may partially reflect the procedure used to define the labels rather than generalizable resolution ability.

Common failure mode: silently changing preprocessing

If production normalization changes from:

NFC

to:

NFKC + case folding

comparison distributions can change even though the probabilistic model parameters remain unchanged.

Preprocessing versions should therefore be part of model versioning.

What should be logged?

For every decision, a production system should ideally retain enough information to reconstruct:

  • which blocking rules generated the pair;
  • the normalized field values;
  • comparison levels;
  • model version;
  • match score or probability;
  • decision threshold;
  • manual-review outcome;
  • cluster operation.

This makes later audit and error analysis possible.

Model drift

The distribution of records can change through:

  • new sources;
  • new countries;
  • changes in data-entry software;
  • new normalization procedures;
  • population shifts;
  • changes in identifier availability.

Therefore:

P(γ | M)

and

P(γ | U)

may change over time.

Linkage systems need monitoring, not one-time calibration.

A useful diagnostic decomposition

When an end-to-end match is wrong, classify the failure by stage:

1. parsing error

2. normalization error

3. blocking miss

4. comparator weakness

5. model error

6. threshold error

7. clustering / constraint error

8. bad ground truth

Without this decomposition, every failure tends to be blamed on:

"the similarity algorithm"

even when Levenshtein distance was computed perfectly.

Where Levenshtein distance belongs

The most defensible role for Levenshtein distance in entity resolution is:

one field-level comparator
inside
a larger evidence system

For example:

name comparison
      │
      ├── exact equality
      ├── Levenshtein distance
      ├── Jaro-Winkler
      └── phonetic signal

address comparison
      │
      ├── structured tokens
      ├── postcode
      └── geographic evidence

date comparison
      │
      ├── exact date
      └── component-level error

             ↓

      linkage model

This architecture respects what edit distance actually measures: the cost of transforming one sequence representation into another.

It does not ask edit distance to answer a fundamentally different question:

Are these two records
the same entity?

The deeper statistical lesson

Entity resolution is an inference problem under uncertainty.

The observed data are imperfect manifestations of an underlying identity.

REAL ENTITY
     │
     ├── source A
     │      ↓
     │   noisy record
     │
     ├── source B
     │      ↓
     │   noisy record
     │
     └── source C
            ↓
         noisy record

The task is therefore not merely:

measure similarity

but:

infer the latent entity
that most plausibly explains
the observed records

Summary

Entity resolution and record linkage identify records that correspond to the same underlying real-world entity despite missing values, formatting differences, typographical errors, stale attributes, duplicated records, and other forms of data corruption.

Levenshtein distance can be valuable in this process, particularly for noisy textual fields such as names. But edit distance measures string transformation cost, not identity. A small character distance is only one piece of evidence and should normally be interpreted together with other fields.

A robust linkage architecture begins with representation and standardization, followed by high-recall candidate generation. Blocking is essential at scale because exhaustive pair comparison grows quadratically or as the cross-product of the input files. Any true match removed during blocking cannot be recovered by later modeling.

Candidate pairs are then converted into comparison vectors. Different attributes require different comparison functions: textual edit distance for some names, exact comparisons for identifiers, structured address comparators, numeric differences, geographic distance, date logic, and domain-specific methods.

The classical Fellegi–Sunter framework treats these comparisons as statistical evidence. Agreement patterns common among true matches but rare among non-matches receive large positive evidence weights. Common agreements provide less evidence. This explains why exact agreement on a rare surname can be much more informative than exact agreement on a common one.

Simple Fellegi–Sunter implementations often use conditional independence to combine field weights, but real record attributes can be correlated. City and postcode, for example, can contain overlapping evidence. Missingness, source-specific error, and blocking-induced candidate distributions also complicate probability estimation.

Modern entity resolution extends beyond independent pair classification. Bipartite linkage can impose one-to-one constraints, Bayesian methods can quantify uncertainty over entire matchings, and multi-record entity resolution must ensure that final clusters obey coherent identity structure.

Pairwise match quality and entity quality are not equivalent. A single false-positive edge can join two otherwise correct clusters, while missed edges can fragment one entity into several. Evaluation should therefore examine both pairwise errors and entity-level overmerging and undermerging.

The most important implementation discipline is to preserve stage boundaries:

representation
→
candidate generation
→
field comparison
→
evidence combination
→
decision
→
entity structure

When these layers are kept separate, Levenshtein distance becomes a precise and useful component of entity resolution rather than an overloaded similarity score asked to infer identity by itself.
