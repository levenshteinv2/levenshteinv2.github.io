Privacy-Preserving Record Linkage

Privacy-preserving record linkage, usually abbreviated PPRL, is the problem of identifying records that refer to the same real-world entity across separately held databases while limiting disclosure of the sensitive identifying information required to perform that linkage.

The underlying record-linkage problem is already difficult:

DATABASE A

Jonathan A. Smith
1984-07-16
42 West Harrison Street


DATABASE B

Jonathon Smith
16/07/1984
42 W Harrison St

A conventional entity-resolution and record-linkage system can inspect these values directly, normalize them, compare them with several string and field-specific comparators, and combine the resulting evidence.

A privacy-preserving system faces an additional constraint:

perform useful linkage

WITHOUT

unnecessarily revealing
the underlying identifiers

This creates a fundamental tension.

The attributes that make records linkable are often the same attributes that make individuals identifiable:

  • names;
  • addresses;
  • dates of birth;
  • telephone numbers;
  • email addresses;
  • government or organizational identifiers;
  • combinations of quasi-identifying attributes.

At the same time, simply replacing every value with an ordinary cryptographic digest eliminates much of the error tolerance required for realistic record linkage.

The central PPRL problem can therefore be stated as:

How much information about similarity must a protocol preserve in order to link noisy records, and how much privacy is lost by preserving that information?

That trade-off—between linkage quality, privacy, and scalability—is the organizing principle of privacy-preserving record linkage.

The basic two-party problem

Suppose organization A owns:

D_A

and organization B owns:

D_B

Each database contains records about people.

The organizations want to determine:

M
=
{
(a,b) :
a ∈ D_A,
b ∈ D_B,
a and b refer to
the same person
}

without simply exchanging:

names
addresses
dates of birth
telephone numbers
...

in clear text.

This may arise in:

  • health research;
  • patient matching;
  • population statistics;
  • administrative-data integration;
  • social-science research;
  • fraud detection;
  • cross-organizational analytics.

PPRL is still record linkage

Privacy technology does not remove the underlying matching problem.

A complete system still needs:

representation
       ↓
normalization
       ↓
candidate generation
       ↓
field comparison
       ↓
evidence combination
       ↓
match classification

PPRL adds another requirement:

protect sensitive information
at every stage
where information crosses
a trust boundary

Privacy-preserving does not mean information-free

A linkage system cannot generally reveal nothing.

At minimum, useful linkage produces some output such as:

record A17
matches
record B901

That result itself reveals information.

For example, if database B represents:

patients at a specialist clinic

then learning that a person appears in B may disclose something about that person's relationship with the clinic.

Therefore a rigorous privacy analysis must distinguish:

INPUT PRIVACY

What can parties learn
about raw identifiers?


INTERMEDIATE LEAKAGE

What can be inferred from
encodings, blocks, scores,
frequencies, or protocol messages?


OUTPUT LEAKAGE

What does the final
linkage result itself reveal?

There is no meaningful privacy claim without a threat model

Statements such as:

"the identifiers are encrypted"

or

"the linkage is privacy preserving"

are incomplete unless they specify:

  • which parties are trusted;
  • what information each party sees;
  • which parties may collude;
  • what auxiliary information an attacker possesses;
  • whether participants follow the protocol;
  • which outputs are revealed;
  • what computational assumptions protect the protocol.

Common adversary models

Honest-but-curious or semi-honest

A semi-honest participant follows the prescribed protocol but tries to infer additional information from everything it legitimately observes.

follow protocol
       +
inspect all available
messages and outputs

Many secure-computation protocols are first analyzed under this model because it is substantially easier to protect against than arbitrary malicious behavior.

Malicious adversary

A malicious party can deviate from the protocol.

It might:

  • construct adversarial inputs;
  • send malformed messages;
  • repeat queries strategically;
  • attempt to manipulate candidate sets;
  • collude with another participant.

Security against malicious behavior generally requires stronger cryptographic protocols and additional computation.

External attacker

An external adversary may obtain:

  • encoded linkage records;
  • configuration information;
  • public population statistics;
  • candidate dictionaries;
  • known plaintext examples.

The attacker does not necessarily need access to the original organizations.

Curious linkage unit

Some architectures send protected records to a third-party linkage service.

That service may follow the protocol faithfully yet still have incentives or opportunities to infer underlying identifiers from the supplied representations.

The parties matter as much as the encoding

A typical architecture may contain:

DATA CUSTODIAN A
      │
      ▼
encoded identifiers
      │
      │
      ▼
LINKAGE UNIT
      ▲
      │
encoded identifiers
      │
DATA CUSTODIAN B

Another architecture may avoid a central linkage unit:

PARTY A
   │
   │ secure protocol
   │
   ▼
cryptographic computation
   ▲
   │
   │
PARTY B

The security properties are fundamentally different.

Pseudonymization is not the same as anonymization

Suppose an identifier is replaced with:

patient_91b7023

This may reduce direct exposure, but the value can still support:

  • longitudinal tracking;
  • linkage across databases;
  • association with sensitive attributes;
  • reidentification when auxiliary data are available.

PPRL encodings should therefore not automatically be described as anonymous simply because the original name is no longer readable.

Exact cryptographic matching is the easy case

Suppose both organizations contain an exact stable identifier:

987654321

and both can compute a keyed cryptographic representation:

HMAC_K(
  "987654321"
)

Then equality testing is straightforward:

HMAC_K(x)
=
HMAC_K(y)

⇒
x = y

with overwhelming probability
under the cryptographic construction

assuming both sides use the same canonical input representation and secret key.

But exact cryptographic equality is brittle

Compare:

Jonathan Smith

Jonathon Smith

A cryptographic hash deliberately exhibits an avalanche effect:

small input change
        ↓
unrelated-looking digest

Therefore:

H(x)
and
H(y)

do not preserve the fact that:

Levenshtein(x,y) = 1

or that the names are otherwise similar.

This is one of the core reasons PPRL is more difficult than ordinary pseudonymization.

The privacy-utility contradiction

Cryptography often tries to make:

similar plaintexts
→
unrelated ciphertexts

while approximate record linkage wants:

similar identifiers
→
recognizably similar
comparison representations

Those goals pull in opposite directions.

Bloom-filter PPRL

One of the most influential approximate PPRL approaches was introduced by Schnell, Bachteler, and Reiher in 2009.

Their method represents q-grams from identifying strings using Bloom filters, allowing approximate comparison after the original identifiers have been transformed.

Schnell, Bachteler and Reiher, 2009.

What is a Bloom filter?

A Bloom filter is a bit array:

B =
[b₀,b₁,...,bₗ₋₁]

where

bᵢ ∈ {0,1}

Initially:

B =
000000000000...

Elements are mapped through several hash functions to positions in the array.

Those positions are set to:

1

q-gram encoding

Suppose:

s =
"SMITH"

q = 2

Then a simple bigram decomposition may contain:

SM
MI
IT
TH

For each q-gram:

g

several keyed hash outputs determine bit positions:

h₁(g)
h₂(g)
...
hₖ(g)

The resulting Bloom filter represents the set of encoded q-grams indirectly through its bit pattern.

Example

Conceptually:

"SMITH"

SM ─┐
MI ─┼──► keyed hashing ──► bit positions
IT ─┤
TH ─┘

                              ↓

B =
001010001101001...

A similar name:

"SMYTH"

shares some q-grams and therefore tends to share some encoded bit positions.

This is not ordinary encryption

Bloom-filter PPRL is frequently described in older literature using terms such as:

encrypted identifiers

But a similarity-preserving Bloom-filter representation should not be confused with semantically secure encryption.

Its usefulness depends precisely on preserving structural information about the input.

That structural information is also what creates privacy risk.

Bloom-filter similarity

Let:

A =
set of 1-bit positions
in Bloom filter A

B =
set of 1-bit positions
in Bloom filter B

A common similarity measure is the Sørensen-Dice coefficient:

Dice(A,B)
=
2|A ∩ B|
─────────
|A| + |B|

with:

0 ≤ Dice ≤ 1

A value closer to:

1

indicates greater Bloom-filter overlap.

Bloom-filter similarity is not Levenshtein distance

This distinction is essential.

A Bloom-filter q-gram comparison measures:

overlap in encoded
local substrings

whereas Levenshtein distance measures:

minimum insertion,
deletion, and substitution
edit cost

between ordered sequences.

The two measures can correlate for typographical variation, but:

Bloom Dice similarity
≠
Levenshtein distance

Why q-grams provide error tolerance

Consider:

SMITH
SMYTH

Only part of the local q-gram structure changes.

Several q-grams survive unchanged, producing overlap in their encoded representations.

This allows approximate matching without revealing the clear-text names directly.

Attribute-level Bloom filters

One architecture creates a separate Bloom filter for each field:

first_name
   ↓
BF_first


last_name
   ↓
BF_last


address
   ↓
BF_address

This makes field-specific comparison easy.

It also exposes more field-specific structure to a linkage party or attacker.

Cryptographic long-term keys

A cryptographic long-term key, or CLK, combines information from several identifying fields into one composite Bloom-filter representation.

Conceptually:

first name ─┐
surname ────┤
DOB ────────┼──► q-grams / encoding
address ────┤
postcode ───┘

                  ↓

            ONE BIT ARRAY

This reduces some of the directly separable field-level structure found in attribute-level Bloom filters.

Record-level Bloom filters

Record-level Bloom filters similarly combine information from several attributes into one representation, often while allocating different contributions to different fields.

The broad design idea is:

multiple attributes
      ↓
weighted / sampled
bit contributions
      ↓
record-level encoding

Composite representations improve some privacy properties but complicate interpretation

Once many fields are combined into a single vector, it becomes harder to say:

surname matched strongly

but

address disagreed

because field-specific evidence has been compressed together.

Privacy and interpretability can therefore conflict.

Bloom-filter parameters matter

Important parameters include:

q =
q-gram size

l =
Bloom-filter length

k =
number of hash mappings

encoding scheme

normalization rules

field composition

secret key / key derivation

hardening mechanism

These parameters affect:

  • collision rates;
  • similarity behavior;
  • linkage quality;
  • privacy leakage;
  • computational cost.

Bloom collisions complicate similarity

Two distinct q-grams can set the same bit positions.

Therefore observed overlap includes:

true shared q-gram structure

+

hash collisions

This is one reason encoded similarity is not identical to clear-text similarity.

Normalization still happens before privacy encoding

Suppose party A encodes:

José García

while party B encodes a different Unicode representation of the same visible text.

If preprocessing differs, privacy encoding does not repair the inconsistency.

Both parties need compatible:

  • Unicode normalization;
  • case rules;
  • whitespace rules;
  • tokenization;
  • address standards;
  • transliteration policy.

See Unicode and Grapheme-Safe Levenshtein and Text Normalization and Transliteration.

Bloom-filter PPRL can preserve substantial linkage quality

Operational research has shown that Bloom-filter methods can approach clear-text linkage quality under suitable configurations.

A blinded Western Australian evaluation published in 2022 compared Bloom-filter PPRL with conventional unencoded linkage using the same blocking criteria.

The study reported that:

99.3%

of the resulting record groupings were identical between the privacy-preserving and clear-text linkage in that specific experiment.

Randall et al., 2022.

This is strong evidence that approximate PPRL can be operationally useful.

It is not evidence that every Bloom-filter construction is secure.

Similarity preservation creates an attack surface

The central security problem can be stated simply:

if encoded records
retain useful patterns
for matching

then

those patterns may also
be useful for inference

Frequency leakage

Real identifiers are not uniformly distributed.

Examples:

Smith
occurs much more often
than a rare surname

and:

common q-grams
occur more frequently
than rare q-grams

If repeated plaintext structures generate repeated or statistically related encoded structures, an adversary may compare:

encoded frequency distribution

with

known population frequency distribution

to infer likely mappings.

Dictionary attacks

Suppose an attacker possesses:

  • the encoding algorithm;
  • a candidate name dictionary;
  • relevant population information;
  • some knowledge of configuration parameters.

The attacker may attempt to encode candidate values and compare structural patterns with the target data.

A keyed hash makes direct reproduction harder when the key remains secret, but key management and protocol design then become critical.

Constraint and pattern attacks

Kuzu, Kantarcioglu, Durham, and Malin showed that Bloom-filter representations used for private record linkage could be attacked by exploiting structural constraints and frequency information.

Kuzu et al., 2011.

The important lesson is broader than one attack:

A representation does not become cryptographically safe merely because a human cannot visually read the original identifier from it.

Attack knowledge can be surprisingly weak

An adversary may not need:

full plaintext database

if publicly available or externally obtainable information provides:

  • common surname frequencies;
  • common q-gram distributions;
  • typical address patterns;
  • name dictionaries;
  • known records;
  • population structure.

Similarity neighborhoods can leak information

Suppose encoded record:

E₁

is highly similar to:

E₂
E₃
E₄
...

The structure of that neighborhood can itself reveal something about the underlying value.

Modern vulnerability analyses therefore examine more than raw frequency.

They can consider:

  • value frequency;
  • value length;
  • co-occurrence;
  • similarity structure;
  • similarity neighborhoods.

Vidanage et al., 2023.

Attack taxonomies are necessary

Different PPRL methods leak different types of structure.

Vidanage, Ranbaduge, Christen, and Schnell developed a taxonomy of PPRL attacks to classify adversaries, available information, attack objectives, and vulnerabilities.

Vidanage et al., 2022.

Security must be evaluated against attacks, not intuition

A representation that:

"looks random"

to its designer may still expose:

frequency

length

co-occurrence

similarity

neighborhood structure

to a statistical attacker.

Hardening Bloom-filter encodings

A large literature has developed countermeasures intended to reduce exploitable structure.

Examples include:

  • randomized hashing strategies;
  • salting;
  • combining several attributes into one representation;
  • balancing;
  • XOR-based transformations;
  • controlled randomization of bits;
  • other transformation schemes designed against specific attack families.

Salting

A salt modifies the representation process using additional information:

encode(
  qgram,
  secret,
  salt
)

rather than:

encode(
  qgram,
  secret
)

If appropriately constructed, identical q-grams need not generate globally identical patterns across every record.

This disrupts simple frequency alignment.

Salting creates an alignment problem

For two records to remain comparable, their encoding transformations must still be compatible in whatever way the protocol requires.

Therefore salting cannot simply be:

choose independent random salt
for every record

without considering how candidate records will later be compared.

Hardening changes utility

Privacy hardening frequently changes:

bit distributions

similarity behavior

collision behavior

comparability

and can therefore affect linkage quality.

Franke and colleagues systematically evaluated several Bloom-filter hardening methods and emphasized precisely this privacy-versus-linkage-quality trade-off.

Franke et al., 2021.

Hardening is not a substitute for a security proof

A statement such as:

"no known attack works
against our current configuration"

is much weaker than:

"this protocol satisfies
a formal security definition
under stated cryptographic assumptions"

Both kinds of systems can be useful, but their guarantees must not be confused.

The limits of reusable privacy encodings

A persistent identifier representation can become risky when reused across:

  • many linkage projects;
  • many organizations;
  • long periods of time;
  • different data releases.

Repeated observations can allow an attacker to accumulate:

co-occurrence
+
frequency
+
linkage
+
auxiliary information

over time.

Project-specific encodings reduce cross-project linkability

One defensive principle is to avoid creating a universal stable encoding that allows arbitrary parties to correlate the same individual across unrelated projects.

Conceptually:

PROJECT A KEY
      ↓
encoding A


PROJECT B KEY
      ↓
different encoding B

even for the same underlying identifier.

Key separation

Different functions should ideally not reuse one secret indiscriminately.

For example:

blocking key

comparison encoding key

output pseudonym key

can be separated cryptographically and operationally.

Key management is part of PPRL

A technically sophisticated encoding fails if:

secret key
is emailed to participants

or

stored beside encoded data

Security architecture must define:

  • key generation;
  • storage;
  • distribution;
  • rotation;
  • revocation;
  • audit;
  • project termination.

Private set intersection

Private set intersection, or PSI, takes a different cryptographic approach.

Suppose:

A =
{a₁,a₂,...,aₙ}

B =
{b₁,b₂,...,bₘ}

PSI allows parties to learn:

A ∩ B

or some defined function of that intersection while limiting disclosure of nonmatching elements.

Exact PSI is naturally suited to exact identifiers

Suppose:

A contains:
person-identity-token-123

B contains:
person-identity-token-123

Then PSI can detect the common token without either side necessarily revealing all nonmatching tokens.

But ordinary PSI does not solve fuzzy record linkage

Consider:

Jonathan Smith

Jonathon Smith

The strings are:

not equal

so ordinary exact PSI sees two different set elements.

The fundamental mismatch is:

PSI:
set equality


record linkage:
approximate identity evidence

Approximate PSI requires additional machinery

Potential strategies include:

  • generating multiple robust representations;
  • locality-sensitive hashing;
  • private threshold matching;
  • secure computation of similarity functions;
  • hybrid PSI and approximate-retrieval constructions.

LSH plus PSI

Adir and colleagues proposed combining locality-sensitive hashing with private set intersection so that approximately similar records can be mapped into candidate representations before secure intersection.

Their system was designed to preserve much of PSI's privacy structure while allowing variation such as spelling or address changes that defeats exact intersection.

Adir et al., 2022.

Approximate representation introduces a new failure layer

When a fuzzy mapping:

F(x)

is applied before PSI, end-to-end behavior depends on:

quality of F

+
privacy of F

+
security of PSI

A cryptographically secure PSI cannot recover true matches lost by a poor approximate representation.

Secure multiparty computation

Secure multiparty computation, or MPC, takes a more general approach.

Instead of publishing a similarity-preserving representation, the parties jointly compute:

f(x,y)

without directly revealing:

x
or
y

beyond the information permitted by the protocol and output.

Ideal functionality

Conceptually, imagine a perfectly trusted black box:

PARTY A
  │
  │ x
  ▼
┌──────────────┐
│ trusted box  │
│              │
│ compute      │
│ f(x,y)       │
└──────────────┘
  ▲
  │ y
  │
PARTY B

The box reveals only:

f(x,y)

and nothing else.

Secure computation tries to emulate this behavior cryptographically without requiring the parties to trust a real central box.

Possible secure linkage function

For example:

f(a,b)
=
w_name × similarity(name_a,name_b)
+
w_dob × agreement(dob_a,dob_b)
+
w_address × similarity(address_a,address_b)

followed by:

return
score ≥ threshold

The parties can potentially learn only the allowed result rather than each other's raw identifiers.

Garbled circuits

Yao-style garbled circuits provide one major secure two-party computation technique.

A computation is represented as a Boolean circuit, and cryptographic techniques allow the parties to evaluate it without learning the private wire values corresponding to the other party's input.

Approximate record linkage can therefore be implemented as a secure circuit.

Secure computation preserves error tolerance directly

Unlike exact PSI, secure computation can evaluate a fuzzy comparator:

string similarity

field weights

threshold logic

missingness

candidate decisions

without first turning the problem into exact equality.

MainSEL

The Mainzelliste SecureEpiLinker, or MainSEL, provides an important healthcare example.

It implements fault-tolerant PPRL using secure multiparty computation and is designed to avoid sending personal identifying data—even in an encoded Bloom-filter form—to a central linkage party.

The authors describe security under explicit cryptographic assumptions and a semi-honest adversary model.

Stammler et al., 2022.

Cryptographic security has a computational cost

Secure computation can require:

  • substantially more CPU work;
  • additional network communication;
  • multiple protocol rounds;
  • specialized implementations;
  • careful threat-model analysis.

The general trade-off becomes:

stronger cryptographic
privacy guarantees

            ↕


more computational and
operational complexity

Network latency matters in MPC

Two implementations with identical CPU performance can behave very differently when deployed across:

same data center

versus:

organizations separated
by high-latency networks

because cryptographic protocols can involve substantial communication.

Differential privacy and PPRL

Differential privacy, or DP, provides formal guarantees for statistical outputs by limiting how much the presence or absence of one individual's data can influence the distribution of released results.

This is conceptually different from ordinary record linkage.

Record linkage wants to determine:

THIS specific record
matches
THAT specific record

while classical differential privacy is designed to make conclusions about individual contributions difficult when releasing statistical information.

The objectives can conflict

PPRL seeks accurate pair-specific identity decisions:

A17 ↔ B491

Differential privacy often introduces uncertainty deliberately:

true information
+
randomized perturbation

to protect individuals.

DP at the blocking layer is not end-to-end privacy

Differentially privatizing:

or:

blocking statistics

does not protect later individual record encodings if those encodings remain vulnerable to inference.

DP on record encodings can reduce linkage utility

If encoded bits are randomly perturbed:

0 → 1

or

1 → 0

then privacy may improve under a particular formal mechanism, but record similarity is also corrupted.

Too much perturbation creates:

  • false negatives;
  • false positives;
  • unstable similarity scores.

Common failure mode: "We hashed the names, so the data are safe"

Ordinary cryptographic hashing can be attacked when:

  • the input domain is predictable;
  • no secret key is used;
  • the same value always creates the same digest;
  • dictionary enumeration is practical.

Names and dates of birth come from highly structured, enumerable domains.

Common failure mode: calling Bloom filters encryption

Similarity-preserving Bloom encodings intentionally leak structural relationships.

Treating them as equivalent to general-purpose semantically secure encryption leads to incorrect security assumptions.

Common failure mode: using obsolete Bloom-filter constructions unchanged

The PPRL attack literature has evolved substantially since 2009.

A system should not copy:

old parameters
+
old hashing method
+
old encoding structure

without evaluating them against modern attacks and current hardening techniques.

Common failure mode: same PPRL key everywhere

A universal long-term secret increases the damage from:

  • key compromise;
  • cross-project correlation;
  • collusion;
  • long-term observational attacks.

Common failure mode: security through secrecy of the algorithm

A secure architecture should assume an attacker may know:

algorithm

parameter ranges

software implementation

population characteristics

and protect the data through well-defined secrets and security properties rather than obscurity.

Common failure mode: protect comparison but expose blocking

A mathematically secure MPC comparator is not enough if the candidate-generation stage publishes highly identifying block keys.

Common failure mode: protect inputs but expose every similarity score

Suppose a party can repeatedly ask:

similarity(secret_record,
candidate_1)

similarity(secret_record,
candidate_2)

...

Detailed score outputs can become an inference oracle.

Rate limits, output minimization, authentication, and query governance may therefore be part of the privacy design.

Common failure mode: ignore malicious query construction

An attacker may submit strategically selected records intended to probe hidden data.

A semi-honest security argument does not automatically cover this behavior.

Common failure mode: assume a trusted third party cannot be compromised

A trusted linkage unit creates a concentrated store of sensitive or linkable data.

Its security model must include:

  • insider access;
  • credential compromise;
  • logging;
  • backup exposure;
  • retention;
  • secondary use.

Common failure mode: keep encoded linkage files forever

Future attacks improve.

Data considered difficult to decode today may become vulnerable when:

  • new cryptanalytic methods appear;
  • auxiliary datasets become available;
  • keys leak;
  • more observations accumulate.

Retention should therefore be justified by actual operational need.

Common failure mode: evaluate only precision and recall

A PPRL method with:

precision = 99.9%

recall = 99.9%

can still be unacceptable if:

80% of names
can be reidentified

under a plausible attack.

Common failure mode: evaluate only privacy

Conversely, a representation that is impossible to reverse but yields:

recall = 40%

may be unusable for the linkage objective.

Common failure mode: ignore subgroup utility

A privacy mechanism may disproportionately damage linkage for:

  • short names;
  • rare scripts;
  • particular address formats;
  • records with missing attributes.

End-to-end evaluation must be stratified.

Common failure mode: assume differential privacy fixes a vulnerable encoding

Adding random bit perturbation can weaken specific attacks but does not automatically make the complete record-linkage process private.

The protected object, privacy unit, output, and adversary model must be specified.

Common failure mode: blockchain as a privacy substitute

Blockchain can provide properties such as:

  • auditability;
  • append-only logging;
  • distributed consensus.

It does not automatically protect the privacy of the linkage representations stored or exchanged.

A published analysis of a blockchain-based PPRL proposal demonstrated precisely this distinction by identifying privacy weaknesses despite the blockchain layer.

Christen et al., 2022.

Common failure mode: confuse anonymity with unlinkability

A representation may hide:

who the person is

while still allowing:

the same person
to be tracked
across releases

Identity confidentiality and cross-context unlinkability are separate properties.

Common failure mode: use deterministic encodings for rare attributes

Rare deterministic encodings can function almost like fingerprints.

The attacker may not need to invert the transformation if auxiliary data reveal which unique individual must correspond to the unique encoded value.

Common failure mode: ignore missingness

Missing fields themselves create observable patterns:

phone absent

A distinctive missingness pattern can contribute to reidentification or population inference.

Common failure mode: disclose all nonmatches

Sometimes the downstream party needs:

matched records only

There may be no legitimate reason to reveal:

every candidate that failed

or every detailed similarity score.

Common failure mode: secure computation with insecure software engineering

A cryptographic proof does not protect:

  • debug logs containing raw names;
  • temporary plaintext files;
  • memory dumps;
  • weak authentication;
  • misconfigured backups;
  • stolen endpoint credentials.

Summary

Privacy-preserving record linkage allows organizations to identify records belonging to the same real-world entities without simply pooling the sensitive personal identifiers ordinarily required for matching. It is therefore both an entity-resolution problem and an information-security problem.

The central technical difficulty is that realistic records contain errors. Exact cryptographic equality works well when both organizations possess the same stable identifier, but a one-character typo produces an unrelated cryptographic digest. Fuzzy linkage requires some representation or protocol capable of recognizing approximate similarity.

Bloom-filter PPRL addresses this problem by mapping q-grams of identifying strings into bit arrays and comparing overlap between those arrays. The method can preserve useful tolerance for spelling variation, and real-world evaluations have demonstrated linkage quality close to conventional clear-text linkage under suitable configurations.

However, Bloom-filter encodings preserve structural information precisely because that information is needed for matching. Frequency distributions, bit patterns, co-occurrence structure, string length, and similarity neighborhoods can create attack surfaces. A substantial cryptanalytic literature has shown that certain Bloom-filter constructions are vulnerable to reidentification and attribute-inference attacks.

Hardening methods such as randomized hashing, salting, composite encodings, balancing, and other transformations can reduce known vulnerabilities, but hardening must be evaluated against both attacks and linkage-quality loss. Resistance to today's known attacks is not equivalent to a formal cryptographic security proof.

Private set intersection provides stronger cryptographic tools for identifying exact shared values, but conventional PSI does not naturally solve fuzzy record linkage because misspelled names are unequal set elements. Hybrid methods combine approximate representations with PSI, while secure multiparty computation can directly evaluate richer fuzzy matching functions without revealing the raw inputs under the protocol's security assumptions.

Secure computation shifts the trade-off toward stronger formal privacy but introduces additional computational and communication cost. It also does not eliminate the need for candidate generation. Comparing every record pair securely remains infeasible for large databases, which makes privacy-preserving blocking and indexing an essential part of the architecture.

Blocking itself creates privacy risks. Block keys, block sizes, candidate relationships, and frequency information can expose attributes even when the later comparison protocol is cryptographically secure. Privacy must therefore be analyzed across the complete linkage process rather than only at the encoding or comparison stage.

The final linkage output also leaks information. Learning that a record occurs in a second database can reveal sensitive membership. A system should therefore return the minimum information necessary for its downstream purpose—perhaps only an intersection count, perhaps pseudonymous pair mappings, or perhaps a full linked dataset depending on the application.

Differential privacy illustrates the importance of matching a privacy mechanism to the correct objective. Differential privacy provides powerful formal guarantees for particular kinds of statistical releases, but recent PPRL research has shown that perturbing blocks or encoded bits does not automatically protect the actual people being linked and may substantially reduce linkage quality.

A rigorous PPRL design begins with a threat model. It specifies the participating organizations, trusted components, possible collusion, attacker knowledge, allowed outputs, key-management assumptions, retention periods, and security properties. Only then can an encoding or cryptographic protocol be evaluated meaningfully.

The strongest operational discipline is therefore:

define the linkage purpose

→ minimize required data

→ normalize locally

→ define the adversary

→ generate candidates privately

→ compare records under
an explicit privacy model

→ reveal only the
necessary linkage result

→ measure linkage quality

→ attack the privacy mechanism

→ monitor and rotate
the system over time

Within this architecture, Levenshtein distance remains useful for exactly the problem it was designed to solve: quantifying insertion, deletion, and substitution differences between sequence representations. Privacy-preserving record linkage determines how that similarity evidence can be computed without unnecessarily exposing the people represented by those strings.
